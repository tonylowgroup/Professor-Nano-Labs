Entanglement: Correlations No Coin Can Fake

Alice and Bob each measure one spin of an entangled pair, one shot at a time. Each of them sees only coin flips, yet their answers line up in a way no pre-agreed answer sheet can copy. See it, score it, and see why it still cannot send a message.

One entangled pair, two dials

A source sends one spin to Alice and one to Bob, in the singlet state. Each sets a dial: a direction in the x–z plane, at angle \(a\) or \(b\) from z. Each measurement gives spin up along the dial (+1) or down (−1), and nothing in between. For the singlet, quantum mechanics predicts

$$|\Psi^-\rangle=\frac{|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle-|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle}{\sqrt2},\qquad P(A{=}{\pm}1)=P(B{=}{\pm}1)=\tfrac12,\qquad E(a,b)=\langle AB\rangle=-\cos(a-b)$$

Each person alone sees a fair coin. The interest is all in how the two coins line up, the correlation \(E\): −1 when they always disagree, +1 when they always agree. The lab compares the pair with two classical stand-ins: "socks", pairs that are simply opposite along z, and the best possible answer sheet, where each pair carries hidden instructions fixed at the source.

Opposite every time, along every axis.

Set both dials to the same direction. The entangled pair always answers opposite ways, whichever direction you pick. A pair of socks (one up, one down along z) does that along z too, but turn both dials to x and the socks become two unrelated coins.

$$E_{\rm pair}(\theta,\theta)=-1\ \text{for every }\theta,\qquad E_{\rm socks}(\theta,\theta)=-\cos^2\theta$$

A cosine that no answer sheet can follow.

Now turn Bob's dial away from Alice's. Quantum mechanics says the correlation follows a cosine. The best answer sheet, a hidden arrow carried by each pair, gives a straight-line zigzag. They agree at 0°, 90° and 180°; in between the cosine bulges beyond the line, and that bulge is what no classical explanation can produce.

$$E_{\rm quantum}=-\cos\theta,\qquad E_{\rm answer\ sheet}=-\left(1-\frac{2\theta}{\pi}\right),\qquad \theta=|a-b|$$

A game with a hard classical ceiling.

Each round, Alice picks dial \(a\) or \(a'\) at random and Bob picks \(b\) or \(b'\). Their four correlations combine into one score. Any answer sheet, however clever, scores at most 2: try all sixteen. The entangled pair reaches \(2\sqrt2\approx2.83\).

$$S=E(a,b)-E(a,b')+E(a',b)+E(a',b'),\qquad |S|_{\rm classical}\le2,\qquad |S|_{\rm quantum}\le2\sqrt2$$

Alice can steer Bob's spin, but she cannot signal.

Before anything is measured, each spin alone has no direction at all: both arrows sit at the centre. When Alice measures, Bob's spin points opposite to her result along her dial. But Bob does not know her result, so his average stays at the centre whatever Alice does. Only when she tells him do his results line up with hers.

$$\vec r_{B\,|\,A=\pm1}=\mp v\,\hat n(a),\qquad \tfrac12\,\vec r_{B\,|\,+1}+\tfrac12\,\vec r_{B\,|\,-1}=0$$

      Step 2 · Predict, then test

      Predict first

      Load each example, make a prediction, then take some pairs to check it.

      Question 1 · Same dial

      Both dials point along x. Will the entangled pair still give opposite answers? Will the socks?

      Show answer

      The entangled pair: yes, every time, \(E=-1\). The socks: no, they now agree half the time and disagree half the time, \(E=-\cos^2 90°=0\). Socks are correlated only along the one axis they were prepared on; the singlet is correlated along every axis at once. That is the difference between classical correlation and entanglement.

      Question 2 · Same dial

      Look only at Alice's own results. What fraction are +1, and does it change when you move the dials?

      Show answer

      Half, always: about 50% for any dial angle and any source. From her own results alone Alice learns nothing, not even which way the dials point. The information is only in comparing the two lists, which is why Question 8 matters.

      Question 3 · Turn one dial

      The dials are 60° apart. How often do Alice and Bob give the same answer, for the entangled pair and for the best answer sheet?

      Show answer

      Entangled pair: \(\tfrac12(1-\cos60°)=25\%\). Answer sheets: \(\theta/\pi=1/3\approx33\%\). With a thousand pairs each the difference is about four standard deviations: you can tell them apart. The pair agrees less often than any hidden instructions allow.

      Question 4 · Turn one dial

      At which angles do the quantum cosine and the answer-sheet zigzag agree, and where is the gap biggest?

      Show answer

      They agree at 0°, 90° and 180°, where the correlation is −1, 0 and +1. The gap is largest near 40° and 140°, about 0.21 in the correlation. Those in-between angles are where nature beats every possible answer sheet, and where a Bell test sets its dials.

      Question 5 · CHSH game

      Write any answer sheet you like: four ±1 answers. Can it score more than 2?

      Show answer

      No. Every one of the sixteen sheets scores exactly +2 or −2, and a random mix of sheets (which is all the hidden-arrow model is) averages to no more than 2. This is the CHSH form of Bell's inequality.

      Question 6 · CHSH game

      With the dials at 0°, 90°, 45° and 135°, what score does the entangled pair reach? After 10,000 rounds, how sure are you it beats 2?

      Show answer

      \(|S|=2\sqrt2\approx2.83\), the largest any quantum state allows (Tsirelson's bound). After 10,000 rounds the uncertainty is about ±0.03, so the result sits about 30 standard deviations above the classical limit.

      Question 7 · CHSH game

      Mix the pair with noise. Below what purity does it stop beating 2? Is it still entangled there?

      Show answer

      The score is \(2\sqrt2\,v\), so it falls to 2 at \(v=1/\sqrt2\approx0.71\). At \(v=0.6\) it scores about 1.70. Yet the pair stays entangled down to \(v=1/3\): a Bell test can miss entanglement that is really there.

      Question 8 · Steering

      Alice switches her dial between z and x. Can Bob tell which she chose, from his own results alone?

      Show answer

      No. Whatever Alice does, Bob gets +1 half the time and his average arrow stays at the centre. Alice's choice decides which pair of directions Bob's spin is split between, ±x or ±z, but the two halves always average to nothing. Only when Alice sends her results by an ordinary channel can Bob sort his and see the correlation. Entanglement steers; it does not signal.

      The companion Jupyter notebook builds the singlet and the classical stand-ins as density matrices, compares the correlation curves, plays the CHSH game with every answer sheet and with simulated shots, and shows steering with a partial trace. It needs only NumPy and Matplotlib.

      Download notebook .ipynb · 16 KB
      What is being computed?
      • Dials: spin measurements along \(\hat n(\theta)=(\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)\), with outcomes ±1. Angles are measured from z toward x, as on the Bloch spheres of the other labs.
      • Entangled pair: the singlet mixed with white noise, \(\rho=v|\Psi^-\rangle\langle\Psi^-|+(1-v)I/4\), so \(P(A,B)=\tfrac14[1-ABv\cos(a-b)]\). It is entangled for \(v>1/3\) and violates CHSH for \(v>1/\sqrt2\).
      • Socks: half \(|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle\), half \(|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle\) along z, a separable mixture: \(E=-\cos a\cos b\).
      • Answer sheets: Bell's hidden-arrow model. Each pair carries a random direction \(\lambda\) in the x–z plane; Alice answers \(\operatorname{sign}\cos(a-\lambda)\) and Bob \(-\operatorname{sign}\cos(b-\lambda)\). It reproduces perfect anticorrelation at equal dials and gives \(E=-(1-2\theta/\pi)\), and it reaches the classical CHSH limit of 2 at the best dials. "My sheet" is one fixed answer sheet.
      • CHSH rounds: each round picks one of Alice's two dials and one of Bob's at random; each correlation is estimated from its own rounds, with a standard error \(\sqrt{(1-E^2)/N}\), and the errors add in quadrature for \(S\).
      • Steering: Bob's state given Alice's result is found by projecting Alice's spin and tracing it out; averaged over her results it is \(I/2\).
      • Checks: the notebook builds every source as a density matrix in NumPy and agrees with this page to about \(10^{-15}\) (the hidden-arrow model to the accuracy of the numerical integral).
