Physics of the Nanoscale Transistor

In a transistor only tens of nanometres long, electrons fly from source to drain without scattering. The gate sets the height of an energy barrier, and the current is what crosses it from the source minus what crosses back from the drain. Operate one here: see why the current saturates, why 60 mV per decade is a hard limit at room temperature, and what a single channel conducts.

Two seas of electrons and a barrier

The source is grounded, \(V_S=0\): its band edge is \(E_c=0\) and it is filled with electrons up to \(E_F\). A drain voltage \(V_D\) lowers every energy in the drain by \(eV_D\), so its Fermi level is \(E_F'=E_F-eV_D\). The top gate sets the height \(U_0\) of the barrier in the channel. In the Landauer picture, electrons leave the source with occupation \(f_S\) and the drain with \(f_D\), and cross the channel with probability \(T(E_x)\), which depends only on the energy of motion along the channel:

$$I_D=\frac{g_se}{h}\sum_{k_y}\int dE_x\,T(E_x)\big[f_S(E)-f_D(E)\big],\qquad E=E_x+E_{k_y},\quad E_{k_y}=\frac{\hbar^2k_y^2}{2m_y}$$

Summing over the transverse momentum \(k_y\) turns each Fermi function into a Fermi–Dirac integral:

$$I_D=I_0\sqrt{k_BT}\int dE_x\,T(E_x)\left[F_{-1/2}\!\left(\frac{E_F-E_x}{k_BT}\right)-F_{-1/2}\!\left(\frac{E_F-eV_D-E_x}{k_BT}\right)\right],\qquad I_0=\frac{g_se}{h}\frac{W}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\sqrt{m_y}}{\hbar}$$

As in the video, \(E_F=0.2\) eV, \(L=50\) nm and \(g_s=2\). The effective mass is \(m=1.25\,m_e\), which reproduces the video's current values. Currents are per unit width, in µA/µm.

The current is a difference of two flows.

Electrons from the source reach the barrier with occupation \(f_S\), and electrons from the drain with \(f_D\). Below the barrier top they bounce back, unless the barrier is thin enough to tunnel through; above it they cross. With no drain bias the two flows cancel exactly. Lower the drain and a window opens between \(E_F'\) and \(E_F\), where the source has electrons the drain lacks.

$$f_S(E)=\frac{1}{e^{(E-E_F)/k_BT}+1},\qquad f_D(E)=\frac{1}{e^{(E-E_F+eV_D)/k_BT}+1},\qquad T(E_x)\approx\begin{cases}e^{-2L\sqrt{2m(U_0-E_x)}/\hbar}&E_x\lt U_0\\1&E_x\ge U_0\end{cases}$$

Why the current stops growing.

Raising \(V_D\) lowers the drain's Fermi level. At first that widens the window and the current grows in proportion to \(V_D\). Once \(E_F'\) drops below the barrier top, the drain's electrons can no longer get over it. The back-flow dies away, only the flow from the source is left, and the current saturates at a value set by the barrier, not by \(V_D\).

$$I_D=I_{S\to D}-I_{D\to S},\qquad \int_{U_0}^{\infty}F_{-1/2}\!\left(\frac{E_F-E_x}{k_BT}\right)dE_x=k_BT\,F_{1/2}\!\left(\frac{E_F-U_0}{k_BT}\right)\ \Rightarrow\ I_{S\to D}\approx I_0\,(k_BT)^{3/2}F_{1/2}\!\left(\frac{E_F-U_0}{k_BT}\right)$$

Sixty millivolts per decade.

The gate moves the barrier top. With the barrier above \(E_F\), only the Boltzmann tail of the source's electrons can get over it, so every \(\ln(10)\,k_BT\) of extra barrier cuts the current tenfold: about 60 mV per decade at 300 K and 40 mV per decade at 200 K. No gate can do better for electrons that go over a barrier, and a very short channel does worse, because electrons start to tunnel through it.

$$I_D\propto e^{-(U_0-E_F)/k_BT},\qquad S=\frac{dV_G}{d\log_{10}I_D}=\ln(10)\,\frac{k_BT}{e}\approx60\ \text{mV/dec at 300 K}$$

Every channel conducts \(2e^2/h\).

Take the barrier away and apply a tiny drain bias. At low temperature \(F_{-1/2}(x)\to2\sqrt{x/\pi}\), and the current becomes proportional to \(V_D\). The conductance counts the transverse modes below \(E_F\), each worth \(2e^2/h\approx77.5\ \mu\)S, however long the channel is.

$$G=\frac{dI_D}{dV_D}=\frac{e^2}{h}\frac{g_sW}{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{2m_yE_F}}{\hbar}=\frac{g_se^2}{h}M(E_F),\qquad M(E_F)=\frac{Wk_F}{\pi}$$

      Step 2 · Predict, then test

      Predict first

      Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

      Question 1 · Landauer picture

      The gate has pulled the barrier down to 0.10 eV, well below \(E_F\), but \(V_D=0\). How much current flows?

      Show answer

      None. With \(V_D=0\) the two Fermi functions are identical, so at every energy as many electrons cross from the drain as from the source. An open channel needs a difference in occupation, not just a low barrier, to carry current.

      Question 2 · Landauer picture

      The barrier is 0.1 eV above \(E_F\). At what energies do the electrons that carry the current travel?

      Show answer

      Just above the barrier top: 95% of the current flows within \(3k_BT\approx78\) meV of \(U_0\). Only the "hot" electrons in the Boltzmann tail of the source can get over, and their numbers fall off as \(e^{-E/k_BT}\). Electrons below the barrier bounce back.

      Question 3 · Saturation

      With the video's barrier, \(U_0=0.125\) eV, at about what drain voltage does the current stop growing? What value does it settle at?

      Show answer

      Around \(V_D=(E_F-U_0)/e=0.075\) V, when the drain's Fermi level drops to the barrier top. The current is already 82% of its final value there, 99% by 0.15 V, and settles at about 2240 µA/µm. The linear and saturation asymptotes cross a little earlier, at about 0.06 V, because the Fermi functions are smeared by temperature.

      Question 4 · Saturation

      At \(V_D=0.05\) V, what fraction of the source's flow comes back from the drain? And at 0.2 V?

      Show answer

      About 36% at 0.05 V, but only 0.2% at 0.2 V. The flow from the source does not depend on \(V_D\) at all; saturation is just the back-flow from the drain disappearing. That is why the saturation current depends on the barrier and the temperature, but not on \(V_D\).

      Question 5 · Subthreshold

      Raise the barrier from 0.30 to 0.36 eV. By what factor does the current drop at 300 K? At 200 K?

      Show answer

      About 10 times at 300 K (10.1) and 32 times at 200 K. Each \(\ln(10)\,k_BT\), 59.5 meV at 300 K and 39.7 meV at 200 K, costs one decade of current. That is the subthreshold slope: 60 and 40 mV/dec in the video.

      Question 6 · Subthreshold

      The gate only moves the barrier by 0.8 eV per volt. Can a better barrier shape bring the slope back below 60 mV/dec at room temperature?

      Show answer

      No. The barrier still needs 59.5 meV per decade, and now each 59.5 meV costs \(59.5/0.8\approx74\) mV of gate voltage. At 300 K, 60 mV/dec is the best any transistor can do when electrons are thermally excited over a barrier. Beating it needs a different mechanism, such as tunnelling transistors or negative-capacitance gates, or cooling the device.

      Question 7 · Subthreshold

      Shrink the channel to 1.5 nm. What happens to the off current at \(U_0=0.4\) eV, and to the slope?

      Show answer

      The off current rises about 6.5 times, from 0.23 to 1.5 µA/µm, because 85% of it now tunnels straight through the barrier. The slope at 0.4 eV degrades from 59.5 to about 100 mV/dec: the gate loses control. This source-to-drain tunnelling is one of the limits on how short a transistor can be.

      Question 8 · Near equilibrium

      No barrier, low temperature, tiny drain bias. What is the conductance per micrometre of width, and how many channels carry it?

      Show answer

      \(G\approx63\) mS/µm, carried by \(Wk_F/\pi\approx815\) transverse modes per µm, each worth \(2e^2/h=77.5\ \mu\)S. That is a resistance of about 16 Ω·µm even with no scattering at all: the contact resistance of a perfect ballistic channel. At 300 K the conductance is only 0.8% lower, because the source is degenerate.

      What is being computed?
      • Device: the source is grounded with its band edge at 0 and filled to \(E_F\); the drain's levels are lowered by \(eV_D\). The barrier top \(U_0\) is set directly, as on the video's slides; it does not depend on \(V_D\). The effective mass is \(m_x=m_y=1.25\,m_e\), which reproduces the video's plotted currents; \(g_s=2\).
      • Current: the Landauer formula integrated over the transverse momentum, as in the video. Above the barrier \(T=1\) and the energy integral is done exactly, \(\int F_{-1/2}=k_BT\,F_{1/2}\). Below the barrier, the WKB transmission \(e^{-2L\sqrt{2m(U_0-E_x)}/\hbar}\) of a square barrier is integrated numerically. Reflection above the barrier is neglected.
      • Fermi–Dirac integrals: \(F_{-1/2}\) and \(F_{1/2}\) are tabulated in your browser when the page opens, with the exact exponential tail below \(x=-40\) and the Sommerfeld expansion above \(x=80\). They agree with direct numerical integration to better than \(10^{-7}\).
      • Diagram: the electron "balls" fade with the Fermi function; the moving band over the barrier shows the share of the current carried at each energy, \(T(E_x)[F_{-1/2}(\eta_S)-F_{-1/2}(\eta_D)]\). The occupation plots use \(E=E_x\) (\(k_y=0\)), as on the slides.
      • Subthreshold slope: \(S=-dU_0/d\log_{10}I_D\) from the computed current, divided by the gate coupling α to convert barrier energy into gate voltage.
      • Conductance: with no barrier, \(G=I_0\sqrt{k_BT}\,e\,F_{-1/2}(E_F/k_BT)\) exactly; it tends to the video's 0 K formula as \(T\to0\).
      • Checks: every current agrees with a direct numerical integration over both \(E_x\) and \(k_y\), the formula on the video's slide 41, to about \(10^{-5}\) or better.
