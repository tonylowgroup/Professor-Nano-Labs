Divergence and Curl: Reading Maxwell's Equations

Maxwell's equations say only two things about a field: how much of it springs out of each point, and how much it swirls round each point. Drag a flux box and a paddle wheel through a gallery of fields to see both; get Coulomb's law and the field of a wire from them; and see why a field that comes from a hill can never swirl.

Two questions to ask a field

A vector field puts an arrow at every point: the electric field, the magnetic field, the velocity of water. Shrink a box around any point and ask two questions. How much of the field flows out of the box, more than flows in? That is the divergence: the field's sources and sinks. And if you dropped a tiny paddle wheel there, would the field turn it? That is the curl: the field's swirl.

Maxwell's four equations are exactly these two questions, asked of the electric field \(\mathbf E\) and the magnetic field \(\mathbf B\): electric charges are where \(\mathbf E\) springs out; nothing is where \(\mathbf B\) springs out, because there are no magnetic charges; currents, and changing fields, are where the fields swirl.

Vector field
An arrow at every point in space: a direction and a strength, like the wind on a weather map.
Flux
How much field crosses a surface: the outward part of the arrows, added up over the surface. Out counts positive, in negative.
Divergence
The net flux out of a tiny box, per unit of its size. Positive at a source, negative at a sink, zero where whatever flows in flows out again.
Circulation
How much the field pushes you along as you walk round a closed loop: the part of each arrow along your path, added up.
Curl
The circulation round a tiny loop, per unit of its area. A tiny paddle wheel turns at half this rate; positive means anticlockwise.
Gradient
The arrows that point straight uphill on a landscape, longer where the slope is steeper. The electric field of static charges is minus the gradient of the voltage.
The math

In two dimensions, for a field \(\mathbf F=(F_x,F_y)\), with Maxwell's equations below:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}=\lim_{A\to0}\frac{1}{A}\oint\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,ds,\qquad (\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z=\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}=\lim_{A\to0}\frac{1}{A}\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf l$$
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0},\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}$$

The divergence theorem of Gauss and the curl theorem of Stokes turn these into statements about whole surfaces and loops: the flux out of a closed surface equals the divergence added up inside it, and the circulation round a loop equals the curl added up over the area it encloses.

Show

What springs out, and what swirls.

Pick a field and drag the probe through it: a small box with a paddle wheel at its centre. The box adds up how much of the field leaves through its four sides, more than enters: the flux, orange where it leaves and blue where it enters. The paddle wheel turns if the field's pushes on its paddles don't balance; going round the box, that imbalance is the circulation. Divide the flux and the circulation by the box's area and you have the divergence and the curl.

What to notice: the shape of the arrows can fool you. Arrows that all point the same way can still turn the wheel, and arrows that go round in circles may not turn it at all. Shrink the box: for a smooth field, flux ÷ area and circulation ÷ area settle on the divergence and curl at that point.

The math

For a small square of side \(\ell\) centred on \((x,y)\), the flux out through the right side minus the flux in through the left is about \(\ell\,[F_x(x+\ell/2)-F_x(x-\ell/2)]\approx\ell^2\,\partial F_x/\partial x\); the top and bottom give \(\ell^2\,\partial F_y/\partial y\). Going round the square, the bottom and top give \(-\ell^2\,\partial F_x/\partial y\), and the sides \(\ell^2\,\partial F_y/\partial x\):

$$\oint\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,ds\approx\ell^2\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf F,\qquad \oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf l\approx\ell^2\,(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z,\qquad \omega_{\rm wheel}=\tfrac12(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z$$

Count what is inside; the field follows.

Gauss's law is the divergence equation added up over the volume inside a closed surface: the flux of \(\mathbf E\) out of any closed surface is the charge inside, divided by \(\varepsilon_0\). Ampère's law is the curl equation added up over a surface bounded by a loop: the circulation of \(\mathbf B\) round any loop is \(\mu_0\) times the current through it. Move and resize the sphere, or the loop, and watch the total stay fixed while the field on it changes completely.

What to notice: the total jumps only when a charge or a wire crosses the surface. Centre a sphere on one charge and symmetry makes the field the same all over it, so a flux of \(q/\varepsilon_0\) spread over \(4\pi r^2\) gives Coulomb's law; a loop round a wire gives the wire's field the same way.

The math

Integrate \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\varepsilon_0\) over the inside of a sphere of radius \(r\) round a point charge and use the divergence theorem; integrate \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J\) over a disc round a wire and use Stokes's theorem:

$$\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A=\frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}\;\Rightarrow\;E\,4\pi r^2=\frac{q}{\varepsilon_0},\;\;E=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2};\qquad \oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0I\;\Rightarrow\;B=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}$$

For a wire of any shape, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\) lets you write \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\); then, choosing \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0\) (the Coulomb gauge), \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J\) becomes \(\nabla^2\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J\), solved like Coulomb's law, and taking its curl gives the Biot–Savart law, \(d\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{d\mathbf l\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}\).

Downhill never swirls; contours never end.

Start from a landscape with a hill and a valley. Minus its gradient, \(-\nabla\varphi\), points downhill, and makes a field that never swirls: walk round any loop and you come back to the height you started at, so the field did no net work on you. Turn every arrow a quarter turn and they run along the contours instead: a field that never springs out of anywhere. The field crossing any stretch of path now equals the change in height along it, and round a closed loop the height comes back to where it started, so as much crosses in as out.

What to notice: on the downhill field, the paddle wheel never turns, wherever you put it; on the field along the contours, the box never catches any net flux. Each field has the other quantity instead: the downhill field springs out of the hilltop, and the contour field swirls round it.

The math

Both zeros come from the order of partial derivatives not mattering, \(\partial_x\partial_y\varphi=\partial_y\partial_x\varphi\):

$$\nabla\times(\nabla\varphi)=\partial_x\partial_y\varphi-\partial_y\partial_x\varphi=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\big(\nabla\times(\psi\hat{\mathbf z})\big)=\partial_x\partial_y\psi-\partial_y\partial_x\psi=0$$

Read backwards, they are how Maxwell's equations are solved: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\) means \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\) for some vector potential \(\mathbf A\), and in statics \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\) means \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\) for some voltage \(V\).

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · Paddle wheel

        In the shear flow every arrow is horizontal: pointing right above the middle line, left below it, and growing steadily from bottom to top. Put the wheel above the line, where every arrow nearby points right. Does it turn?

        Show answer

        Yes, clockwise. The field pushes harder on the wheel's top paddles than on its bottom ones, so it turns, even though nothing flows in a circle. The curl is \(\partial F_y/\partial x-\partial F_x/\partial y=0-1=-1\) everywhere, the circulation round the box is \(-1\times\) its area (\(-0.36\) for a box 0.6 across), and the wheel turns at half the curl, 0.5 radians per unit time. Think of a stick floating in a river that flows faster on one side: it spins.

        Question 2 · Paddle wheel

        Water circles a drain, faster and faster towards the middle (\(v\propto1/r\)). Put the paddle wheel away from the drain. Does it turn?

        Show answer

        No. The wheel is carried round the drain, but it does not turn about its own axis: the faster water on its inner side is exactly offset by the way the flow curves, so the curl is zero everywhere except at the drain itself. The circulation round any box that misses the drain is 0; round any box that encloses it, it is \(2\pi\approx6.283\), however big or small the box. All the curl is concentrated at one point, like the magnetic field round a thin wire.

        Question 3 · Flux box

        Every arrow in the fading source points outwards, away from the centre. Move the box out to \(x=1.6\). Is more field leaving the box than entering?

        Show answer

        No: less. The arrows point out, but out here they shrink faster than they spread apart, so less field leaves the box than enters it, and the flux is negative: this is a sink. The divergence, \(1.5\,(2-r^2)\,e^{-r^2/2}\), is positive only inside \(r=\sqrt2\approx1.41\); at \(x=1.6\) it is \(-0.23\). Arrows pointing away from a point don't make a source; what matters is whether more leaves than arrives.

        Question 4 · Flux box

        In the saddle, the field rushes in from the top and bottom and out to the left and right. Centre the box on the middle. Does field pile up, or does the wheel turn?

        Show answer

        Neither. With the box 0.6 across, 0.18 flows in through the top, 0.18 through the bottom, and 0.18 out through each side: a net flux of zero, so the divergence is \(1+(-1)=0\). The pushes on the wheel's paddles cancel too, so the curl is zero. A field can look dramatic and still have no sources and no swirl; this one is the flow round the corner of a room.

        Question 5 · Gauss

        A sphere of radius 0.5 sits centred on a point charge. Double its radius to 1. What happens to the flux through it, and to the field on its surface?

        Show answer

        The flux stays exactly the same, \(q/\varepsilon_0\): the same charge is inside. But the surface area grows four times, so the field on it must fall to a quarter, from \(0.318\) to \(0.080\) (in units where \(q/\varepsilon_0=1\)). That is the inverse-square law: \(E\cdot4\pi r^2=q/\varepsilon_0\), so \(E=q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2\). Coulomb's law follows from Gauss's law plus symmetry.

        Question 6 · Gauss

        Now the sphere of radius 1 encloses a positive and a negative charge. The field on the sphere is clearly not zero. What is the total flux?

        Show answer

        Zero. The field leaves the sphere on the side near the positive charge (0.47 outward where it is strongest) and enters on the side near the negative one, by exactly as much. Gauss's law counts only the net charge inside, here \(q-q=0\). Slide the sphere until only one charge is inside and the flux jumps to \(\pm q/\varepsilon_0\).

        Question 7 · Ampère

        A loop sits beside a current-carrying wire without going round it. The magnetic field on the loop isn't zero. What is the circulation round it?

        Show answer

        Zero. On the side of the loop nearer the wire, \(\mathbf B\) runs one way along the loop; on the far side it runs the other way, weaker but along a longer stretch, and the two cancel exactly. Ampère's law counts only the current through the loop. Move the loop to go round the wire, anywhere and at any size, and the circulation is \(\mu_0I\) every time.

        Question 8 · Two zerosChallenge

        The field points downhill from a hill, \(\mathbf F=-\nabla\varphi\). Put the box on the hilltop. Does field leave the box? Does the wheel turn? Try other places too.

        Show answer

        Field leaves the box, about 2.1 for a box 0.6 across, because all the arrows run away downhill from the top: the hilltop is a source. But the wheel never turns, here or anywhere: the circulation is zero for every box, because walking round a loop on a landscape brings you back to the height you started at. That is \(\nabla\times\nabla\varphi=0\). It is why the electric field of static charges, \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\), has no curl; and turned a quarter turn, it is why a field written as a curl, like \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\), never springs out of anywhere.

        What is being computed?
        • Units: lengths and fields in arbitrary units, the picture running from −2 to 2. In tab 2, charges in units where \(q/\varepsilon_0=1\) and currents where \(\mu_0I=1\).
        • The fields: source \((x,y)\), vortex \((-y,x)\), shear \((y,0)\), saddle \((x,-y)\), river \((1-y^2/4,\,0)\), fading source \(1.5\,(x,y)\,e^{-r^2/2}\), line charge \((x,y)/r^2\) and whirlpool \((-y,x)/r^2\). Their divergence and curl are worked out by hand, and the box measures them independently.
        • Flux and circulation: \(\oint\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,ds\) and \(\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf l\) on each side of the box, by 24-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature. For the line charge and the whirlpool, whose fields blow up at the centre, each side is done exactly: for the line charge's flux and the whirlpool's circulation it contributes the angle it subtends at the centre, and for the other quantity a logarithm of distances that cancels round the box. A box whose edge runs through the centre gets the symmetric value, half the total.
        • The paddle wheel turns at half the curl at its centre; the moving dots are carried along by the field, faster where it is stronger.
        • Gauss's law: the field of point charges in three dimensions, \(\mathbf E=q\,\hat{\mathbf r}/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2\), shown in the plane through them. The flux through the sphere is a surface integral; by symmetry about the line from the sphere's centre to each charge it reduces to one integral, done on panels that crowd in where a charge comes close to the surface. It agrees with Gauss's law to better than \(10^{-7}\).
        • Ampère's law: long straight wires along \(z\), \(\mathbf B=\mu_0I\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}/2\pi r\). The circulation is summed over a 1440-sided polygon; along each straight piece, \(\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l\) is exactly \(\mu_0I\) times the angle it subtends at the wire, divided by \(2\pi\).
        • Two zeros: the landscape \(\varphi\) is a hill of height 1.2 at \((-0.8,0.5)\) and a valley of depth 1 at \((0.9,-0.45)\), both Gaussians of width 0.6. The downhill field is \(-\nabla\varphi\); the contour field is \(\nabla\times(\varphi\hat{\mathbf z})=(\partial_y\varphi,-\partial_x\varphi)\). Their divergence and curl are \(-\nabla^2\varphi\) and 0, and 0 and \(-\nabla^2\varphi\).
