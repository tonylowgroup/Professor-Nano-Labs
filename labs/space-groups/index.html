The 230 Space Groups

Every crystal belongs to one of exactly 230 space groups: the complete list of ways a pattern can repeat in three dimensions. Pick a group by its number and watch its operations act on a unit cell, one at a time: rotations and screw axes, mirrors and glide planes, inversion centres. Then read its diagram the way the International Tables draw it, and watch the same operations build real crystals from a handful of atoms.

Moves that leave a crystal unchanged

A symmetry operation is a move that leaves a crystal looking exactly as before: every atom lands on an atom of the same kind. Shifting by a whole unit cell is one, and so are turns about an axis, reflections in a plane and inversion through a point. Combine them with half, third, quarter or sixth steps along the way and you get screw axes and glide planes, which only an infinite repeating pattern can have.

All the operations of a crystal together form its space group. In 1891 Fedorov and Schoenflies, working separately, showed that there are exactly 230 of them. Each has a number and a Hermann–Mauguin symbol such as P2₁/c, whose letters name the lattice and the symmetry along the main directions.

Symmetry operation
A move that maps the crystal onto itself: written \(x\to Wx+w\) in cell coordinates, a matrix \(W\) for the turning or reflecting part and a vector \(w\) for the shift.
Screw axis
Turn about an axis, then slide along it: 2₁ is half a turn and half a cell; 6₃ is a sixth of a turn and half a cell.
Glide plane
Reflect in a plane, then slide parallel to it: by half a cell along an axis (a, b, c glides), along a diagonal (n), by a quarter of a diagonal (d), or in some other direction (g). An e in a group's symbol marks a plane that is two glides at once.
Inversion centre
A point through which every atom has a twin on the opposite side, at the same distance.
General position
A point that sits on no symmetry element. Its copies, one per operation, show the whole group; their number is the group's order in one cell.
Wyckoff position
A set of points on symmetry elements, with fewer copies: an atom at the 8a position of diamond has 8 copies in the cell, not 192.
The math

An operation \((W,w)\) sends \(x\) to \(Wx+w\). Repeating it \(k\) times, where \(W^k=1\), gives a pure translation; a third of it, a half, and so on, is the intrinsic screw or glide part, and what is left fixes the symmetry element in place:

$$w_{\rm intr}=\frac1k\big(w+Ww+\dots+W^{k-1}w\big),\qquad (W-1)\,x_0=-(w-w_{\rm intr}),\qquad \det W=\pm1,\quad \mathrm{tr}\,W=\det W\,\big(1+2\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{n}\big)$$

In cell coordinates \(W\) has whole-number entries, so its trace must be a whole number: \(n\) can only be 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6. That is the crystallographic restriction, and why crystals have no five-fold axes.

Show

One operation at a time.

The cell holds a small handed object, three coloured balls on a stand, at a general position, and one copy of it for every operation of the group. Pick an operation from the list: its symmetry element appears in the cell, and every copy, with the cell itself, moves as one rigid block. A rotation turns them, a mirror flips them into mirror images; a screw turns them and then slides them along its axis, and a glide reflects them and then slides them along its plane. At the end every moved copy sits where the repeating pattern has a copy, in this cell or the next. The Move switch can show a single object, or the copies without the cell.

What to notice: rotations and screws keep the object's handedness; mirrors, glides, inversion and rotoinversions swap it, shown by a white hub instead of a dark one. Groups with no operation of the second kind, only rotations and screws, are the 65 that chiral molecules and crystals such as quartz can have.

The math

The determinant and trace of \(W\) give the kind of operation; the eigenvector of \(W\) with eigenvalue \(+1\) (of \(-W\) for the improper ones) gives the axis, and for a mirror the plane is spanned by the eigenvectors with eigenvalue \(+1\). The list shows each operation's symbol, its element in the notation of the International Tables (\(0,0,z\) is the line along \(c\) through the origin; \(x,\tfrac14,z\) the plane at \(y=\tfrac14\)), and the coordinate triplet:

$$\det W=+1:\ \mathrm{tr}\,W=3,-1,0,1,2\ \Rightarrow\ 1,2,3,4,6;\qquad \det W=-1:\ \mathrm{tr}\,W=-3,1,0,-1,-2\ \Rightarrow\ \bar1,m,\bar3,\bar4,\bar6$$

The group, looking down c.

The International Tables draw each group from above. The general-position diagram shows every copy of one point as a circle, labelled with its height: + is a little above the plane, − a little below, ½+ half a cell higher. A comma inside marks a mirror-image copy. The symmetry-element diagram adds the axes, the planes and the inversion centres in symbols modelled on the International Tables' ones.

What to notice: a 2₁ screw along c moves a copy half a cell up as it turns it, so the copies come in pairs at + and ½+; a plain 2-fold leaves them at the same height. Glide planes are dashed or dotted lines, mirrors solid. Elements that are tilted to the page, like the three-fold axes of a cubic group, are counted but not drawn.

The math

The circle for operation \((W,w)\) sits at \((Wx_0+w)\) brought into the cell, drawn at its \(x\) and \(y\). If \(W\) sends \(z\) to \(\pm z\), its height is \(\pm z_0+w_3\), which the diagram writes as + or − with the fraction in front; otherwise the height depends on \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) too, and is written as a number.

A few atoms, and the group does the rest.

A crystal structure is stored as its space group, its cell, and the atoms of the asymmetric unit: the smallest set from which the group's operations generate everything else. Pick a material and watch: the operations turn one silicon atom into the eight of the diamond cell, two atoms into graphite's layers. Pick an operation and apply it to the whole crystal: every atom lands on an atom of the same kind.

What to notice: an atom on a symmetry element has fewer copies than a general one: its Wyckoff position's multiplicity. Compare diamond, Fd3̄m, with zincblende, F4̄3m: the same atoms, but two kinds of them, and half the operations are lost.

The math

Each atom at \(x_j\) in the asymmetric unit has copies \(Wx_j+w\) for every operation, brought into the cell; copies that coincide are counted once. The number of distinct copies, the multiplicity, is the group's order divided by the order of the atom's site symmetry:

$$\text{multiplicity}=\frac{|G|}{|G_{x_j}|},\qquad\text{e.g. diamond: }\frac{192}{24}=8$$

      Question 1 · Operations

      P2₁/c (No. 14) is the most common space group of all, home to a third of all organic crystals. How many copies of a general point does one cell hold, and what are the operations that make them?

      Show answer

      Four. The identity; a 2₁ screw axis along \(b\), at \(0,y,\tfrac14\): half a turn and half a cell along \(b\); an inversion centre at the origin; and a \(c\)-glide plane at \(y=\tfrac14\): reflect, then slide half a cell along \(c\). Two of the four copies are mirror images of the other two. The symbol says it all: P for a primitive lattice, 2₁/c for a screw axis along \(b\) with a glide plane perpendicular to it.

      Question 2 · Diagram

      P2 (No. 3) and P2₁ (No. 4) both have a two-fold axis along \(b\) and two copies of each point. What is different about where the two copies sit?

      Show answer

      In P2 the second copy is at \((-x, y, -z)\), at the same height along the axis; in P2₁ it is at \((-x, y+\tfrac12, -z)\), half a cell further along \(b\). The 2₁ screw turns and slides. Both have two operations, and from far away they look alike, but the screw axis makes a helix, and in a diffraction pattern it wipes out every other spot along \(b\) (the reflections \(0k0\) with \(k\) odd).

      Question 3 · Operations

      Pnma (No. 62) is the group of many perovskites and of olivine. Its symbol has three letters after P. What does each one stand for?

      Show answer

      Each letter is the plane perpendicular to one axis: an \(n\)-glide perpendicular to \(a\) (at \(x=\tfrac14\), sliding along the diagonal \((0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\)), a mirror perpendicular to \(b\) (at \(y=\tfrac14\)), and an \(a\)-glide perpendicular to \(c\) (at \(z=\tfrac14\), sliding half a cell along \(a\)). Together with three 2₁ screw axes and an inversion centre at the origin they give eight operations, and eight copies of a general point.

      Question 4 · Operations

      Fm3̄m (No. 225), the group of rock salt and of copper, has the full symmetry of a cube, 48 operations. How many copies of a general point are there in its conventional cubic cell?

      Show answer

      192: the 48 operations of the cube, each combined with the four lattice points of the face-centred cell, \((0,0,0)\), \((0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), \((\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12)\), \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)\). The list shows the 48 once, as the International Tables do, and the centring translations at the end. Rock salt has only 8 atoms in the same cell, because each sits on a site that 48 operations leave in place: \(192/48=4\) of each kind.

      Question 5 · Crystals

      Diamond and zincblende have the same arrangement of atoms; in zincblende, alternate atoms are gallium and arsenic. What symmetry does that lose?

      Show answer

      Every operation that swaps the two sublattices, half of them: the group drops from Fd3̄m (No. 227), with 192 operations in the cell, to F4̄3m (No. 216), with 96. Among the losses are all the inversion centres, which sit midway between neighbouring atoms in diamond. No inversion centre is what lets zincblende semiconductors such as GaAs be piezoelectric and generate second harmonics of light.

      Question 6 · Crystals

      Graphite stacks graphene sheets in an ABAB pattern. Apply the 6₃ screw operation to the whole crystal. Where does a sheet go?

      Show answer

      Onto the next sheet. A 6₃ screw turns by 60° about the axis and slides half a cell along \(c\); the axis runs through atoms that sit directly above one another in neighbouring sheets, and turning an A sheet by 60° about it gives the B pattern, which the slide lifts to where the B sheet is. Every atom lands on an atom. Without the screw axis, AA stacking, the group would be P6/mmm, and graphite would have one sheet per cell instead of two.

      Question 7 · Crystals

      Look through the operations of α-quartz, P3₁21 (No. 152). Is there a mirror, a glide plane or an inversion centre? What does that mean for the crystal?

      Show answer

      None: only the identity, 3₁ screws and two-fold axes, six operations, all of them rotations. So the crystal is chiral: its mirror image cannot be turned into it. Left- and right-handed quartz both exist, in the pair of groups P3₁21 and P3₂21 (No. 154), and they rotate the polarisation of light in opposite directions. Only 65 of the 230 groups contain no mirror-type operation at all.

      Question 8 · SymmetryChallenge

      The highest rotation in any of the 230 groups is six-fold. Why can't a crystal have a five-fold or an eight-fold axis?

      Show answer

      In cell coordinates every operation is a matrix of whole numbers, because it must send lattice points to lattice points; so its trace is a whole number. A rotation by \(2\pi/n\) has trace \(1+2\cos(2\pi/n)\), which is a whole number only for \(n=1,2,3,4,6\): for \(n=5\) it is 1.618, the golden ratio. Five-fold patterns can be ordered without repeating, as in quasicrystals, but they cannot be periodic.

      What is being computed?
      • The groups: all 230, in the standard settings of the International Tables: unique axis \(b\) for monoclinic groups, origin choice 2 (an inversion centre at the origin) for the 24 groups that have two, and hexagonal axes for the rhombohedral groups. The operations were taken from the gemmi crystallography library; for each, the coset representatives are stored and the lattice centring adds the rest. Five orthorhombic groups are shown by their current symbols (Aem2, Aea2, Cmce, Cmme, Ccce).
      • The classification: from the determinant and trace of \(W\); the axis from the null space of \(W-1\) (or \(W+1\)); the screw or glide part by averaging \(w\) over one cycle of \(W\); the element's position by solving \((W-1)x_0=-(w-w_{\rm intr})\). Glide vectors are reduced by lattice translations lying in the plane, and named \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), \(n\) or \(d\), or \(g\) for any other glide direction. The description of each operation gives its own slide, and says when it is the same plane as a conventional glide one lattice translation away. A separate Python check confirmed every one of the 2609 operations, and that every screw axis and glide plane in every Hermann–Mauguin symbol appears in its group.
      • The pictures: the cell shapes are only illustrations: the group fixes which shapes are allowed (cubic, hexagonal...), not the lengths. The handed object sits at the general position \((0.137, 0.229, 0.083)\). The animations interpolate each operation: a turn about the axis, a reflection squeezed through the plane, an inversion squeezed through the centre; screws and glides in two steps, the turn or reflection first and then the slide. Every operation comes with copies shifted by whole cells; the animation of the whole block uses the one that keeps the moved cell overlapping the original most, with screws sliding forwards along their axis, and selecting a screw stands its axis upright.
      • The stereogram: the point group, the operations with their shifts removed, acting on one direction; upper-hemisphere poles are dots, lower ones circles.
      • The crystals: published cell parameters and Wyckoff positions; the bonds are drawn between atoms closer than a cut-off for each material, and the computed bond lengths (2.82 Å in NaCl, 2.35 Å in silicon, 1.42 Å in graphite, 1.60 and 1.61 Å for Si–O in quartz) match the measured ones.
