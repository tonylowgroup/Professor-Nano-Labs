Trapped-Ion Qubit

A single ion held in a trap stores a qubit in two of its energy levels, and laser pulses are its gates. Drive it, time the pulses, and see how noise and energy loss slowly erase what it stores.

A two-level ion driven by a laser

Two long-lived levels of the ion store the qubit: \(|0\rangle\), the ground state, and \(|1\rangle\), an excited state. A laser tuned close to the transition frequency \(\omega_0\) drives the ion between them. In a frame that rotates with the laser, and keeping only the near-resonant terms (the rotating-wave approximation), the ion feels a fixed "drive vector" \(\vec W\), and its Bloch vector precesses about it like a spinning top:

$$H=\frac{\hbar}{2}\,\vec W\cdot\vec\sigma,\qquad \vec W=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\cos\phi\\ \Omega\sin\phi\\ \Delta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Delta=\omega_{\rm laser}-\omega_0 ,\qquad \frac{d\vec r}{dt}=\vec W\times\vec r-\begin{pmatrix}r_x/T_2\\ r_y/T_2\\ (r_z-1)/T_1\end{pmatrix}$$

The Rabi frequency \(\Omega\) is set by the laser's strength, \(\phi\) by its phase and \(\Delta\) by its tuning. The last term adds the two kinds of decay: energy loss back to the ground state with time \(T_1\), and loss of phase with time \(T_2\). To read the qubit out, a second laser makes the ion glow only when it is in \(|0\rangle\); repeating the experiment gives the probability \(P(1)\) plotted throughout this lab.

Switch the laser on. The ion flops between its two levels.

On resonance the arrow swings all the way from \(|0\rangle\) to \(|1\rangle\) and back. Detune the laser and the drive axis tilts out of the equator: the swing gets faster but never reaches \(|1\rangle\). A pulse that stops after half a swing is a π pulse, the ion's X gate.

$$P_1(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\,\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\;t}{2}\right),\qquad t_\pi=\frac{\pi}{\Omega}$$

Two short pulses and a wait: the most precise clock there is.

A π/2 pulse puts the arrow on the equator. During the wait, with the laser off, it precesses at the detuning; a second π/2 pulse turns that phase into a probability. Slow field noise blurs the result, and a π pulse in the middle of the wait, a spin echo, undoes it.

$$P_1^{\rm Ramsey}\approx\tfrac12\left[1+\cos(\Delta\tau)\,e^{-\tau/T_2}\,e^{-\sigma^2\tau^2/2}\right],\qquad P_1^{\rm echo}\approx\tfrac12\left[1+e^{-\tau/T_2}\right]$$

Leave the ion alone. It forgets.

After a pulse, energy loss pulls the arrow back toward the ground state with time \(T_1\), and dephasing shrinks its part in the equator with time \(T_2\). Either way the arrow moves inside the sphere: the ion is now in a mixed state, described by a density matrix rather than a single ket.

$$\rho=\frac{I+\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma}{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1-P_1&\rho_{01}\\ \rho_{01}^{*}&P_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad P_1(t)=P_1(0)\,e^{-t/T_1},\qquad |\rho_{01}(t)|=|\rho_{01}(0)|\,e^{-t/T_2},\qquad T_2\le 2T_1$$

      Step 2 · Predict, then test

      Predict first

      Load each example, make a prediction, then press Play or move a slider to check it.

      Question 1 · Rabi

      How long must the laser stay on to put the ion in |1⟩ for certain? What does a pulse twice as long do?

      Show answer

      A π pulse: \(t_\pi=\pi/\Omega=1/(2\times100\ \text{kHz})=5\ \mu\text{s}\). Twice as long, a 2π pulse, brings the arrow all the way round to \(|0\rangle\) again. The ion is back where it started, though its amplitudes have changed sign, the same −1 as a 360° rotation in the Bloch-sphere lab.

      Question 2 · Rabi

      Detune the laser by exactly the Rabi frequency, 100 kHz. Will the ion ever be fully excited? Does it flop faster or slower than on resonance?

      Show answer

      Never. P(1) peaks at \(\Omega^2/(\Omega^2+\Delta^2)=1/2\). It flops faster, at \(\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}/2\pi\approx141\) kHz, because the arrow now circles a drive axis tilted 45° out of the equator, and that circle never reaches the south pole.

      Question 3 · Rabi

      A π pulse excites the ion only when the laser is close to resonance. Halve the Rabi frequency from 100 to 50 kHz, so the π pulse lasts twice as long. Does the line in the spectrum get wider or narrower?

      Show answer

      Narrower, by a factor of two. The first zeros sit at \(\Delta=\pm\sqrt3\,\Omega\): ±173 kHz at 100 kHz and ±87 kHz at 50 kHz. A longer, weaker pulse resolves frequency more finely, the same trade-off as in a Fourier transform. Precision spectroscopy uses long pulses for exactly this reason.

      Question 4 · Ramsey

      With a 10 kHz detuning and a 50 µs wait, how far round the equator does the arrow get during the wait? What is P(1) at the end? How far apart are the fringes when you scan the detuning?

      Show answer

      \(\Delta\tau=2\pi\times10\ \text{kHz}\times50\ \mu\text{s}=\pi\): exactly half a turn, so the second π/2 pulse sends the arrow back up and P(1) ends close to 0 (about 0.01, because the detuning also acts during the pulses). Scanning the detuning gives fringes \(1/\tau=20\) kHz apart, far narrower than the π-pulse line. That is why atomic clocks use Ramsey's method.

      Question 5 · Ramsey

      Now the detuning drifts from shot to shot by about 3 kHz. The Ramsey fringes against wait time fade out. Will a spin echo bring them back?

      Show answer

      Yes, completely. Each shot has a slightly different detuning, so the arrows fan out around the equator and their average shrinks: the contrast falls as \(e^{-\sigma^2\tau^2/2}\), to 0.64 at 50 µs and 0.17 at 100 µs. The echo's π pulse flips the fan over, so the second half of the wait undoes the first, whatever each shot's detuning. Watch the thin arrows close up again. With no other noise, the echo ends within about 1% of P(1) = 1 for every wait; the small shortfall comes from the detuning acting during the pulses themselves.

      Question 6 · Ramsey

      Keep the field noise and add dephasing with T₂ = 100 µs. Does the echo still rescue everything?

      Show answer

      No. The echo cancels only noise that stays the same throughout a shot. T₂ describes fast, random phase kicks that differ between the two halves of the wait, so they do not cancel. The echo signal now decays roughly as \(\tfrac12(1+e^{-\tau/T_2})\), to about 0.67 at τ = 100 µs.

      Question 7 · T₁ and T₂

      A π pulse puts the ion in |1⟩, then it is left alone. With T₁ = 300 µs, what is P(1) after 300 µs? Where does the arrow end up much later, and is that a mixed state?

      Show answer

      \(e^{-1}\approx0.37\). The arrow slides straight up the z axis, shrinking to nothing as it passes the centre, and ends at \(|0\rangle\) on the surface. Energy loss eventually returns the ion to a pure state, the ground state, but on the way it passes through mixed states, with a purity of only 0.5 when P(1) = 0.5.

      Question 8 · T₁ and T₂

      T₂ is set to ∞, so there is no dephasing at all. After the π/2 pulse, will the arrow's part in the equator still shrink?

      Show answer

      Yes, with \(T_2=2T_1=600\ \mu\text{s}\). Every decay from \(|1\rangle\) to \(|0\rangle\) also destroys the phase between them, so energy loss alone sets \(T_2\le2T_1\). The lab caps T₂ at that limit; a larger value would make the density matrix unphysical.

      What is being computed?
      • Model: one two-level ion in the frame rotating with the laser, after the rotating-wave approximation. \(|0\rangle\) is the ground state at the north pole. The ion's motion in the trap, other levels and laser intensity noise are left out. All lasers here drive the qubit transition directly.
      • Evolution: the Bloch equations above, linear in \(\vec r\) for constant \(\vec W\), are solved exactly for each pulse and wait with a 4 × 4 matrix exponential. Waits use the closed form: precession at Δ, transverse decay \(e^{-t/T_2}\), and \(r_z\) relaxing to +1 as \(e^{-t/T_1}\). This is equivalent to the Lindblad master equation with energy loss at rate \(1/T_1\) and pure dephasing at rate \(1/T_2-1/2T_1\). T₂ is capped at \(2T_1\).
      • Field noise: a Gaussian spread of standard deviation σ in the detuning, constant during a shot and random between shots. The plotted state is the average over 41 detunings evenly spaced over ±4σ with Gaussian weights; the thin arrows on the sphere are some of those shots.
      • Sequences: Ramsey is π/2 – wait τ – π/2, all pulses about x. The echo is π/2 – τ/2 – π – τ/2 – π/2, with the last pulse about −x so that an ideal echo ends in \(|1\rangle\) like an ideal Ramsey sequence on resonance. Pulse lengths are \(t_\pi=\pi/\Omega\) and \(t_\pi/2\), and decay and detuning act during the pulses too.
      • Scale: the noise sliders reach far shorter times than a good experiment, so the decay fits on the plots. In real trapped-ion qubits T₁ ranges from about a second (optical qubits) to effectively forever (hyperfine qubits), and T₂ from milliseconds to many seconds.
      • Checks: the notebook solves the same problems with the Lindblad master equation for the density matrix in NumPy. It agrees with this page to about \(10^{-14}\).
