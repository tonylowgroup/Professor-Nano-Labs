Magnetization Dynamics and the LLG Equation

Put a nanomagnet in a field and it does not simply swing round to point along it: it spins around the field like a top, and only damping lets it settle. Watch a magnet precess, spiral in and flip between the two states of a memory bit, computed live from the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation, then write one bit of an MRAM array without disturbing its neighbours.

A magnet behaves like a spinning top

In a small enough magnet all the electron spins point together, so its state is a single arrow: the direction of the magnetization, \(\mathbf m=\mathbf M/M_S\), a point on a sphere. A field exerts a torque on it, and because the magnetization carries spin angular momentum, the torque turns it around the field instead of toward it. Damping slowly drains its energy and lets it spiral in. The Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert (LLG) equation has one term for each:

$$\frac{d\mathbf m}{dt}=-\gamma\,\mathbf m\times\mathbf H_{\rm eff}+\alpha\,\mathbf m\times\frac{d\mathbf m}{dt},\qquad \mathbf H_{\rm eff}=-\frac{1}{\mu_0M_S}\frac{\partial E}{\partial\mathbf m}$$

The first term is the field torque, which conserves energy; the second is the damping torque, which dissipates it. \(\gamma=\mu_0\gamma_e=2.21\times10^5\) m A⁻¹ s⁻¹ sets the pace (28 GHz per tesla) and \(\alpha\) is the damping constant. The effective field is the slope of the energy: an applied field gives \(E=-\mu_0M_S\,\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf H_{\rm ext}\) and uniaxial anisotropy gives \(E=K_{\rm eff}\big[1-(\mathbf m\cdot\hat{\mathbf z})^2\big]\).

The field turns the magnetization; it does not pull it.

Without damping, the torque \(-\gamma\,\mathbf m\times\mathbf H\) is perpendicular to both \(\mathbf m\) and \(\mathbf H\). It carries \(\mathbf m\) around a cone of fixed angle \(\theta\), so the energy never changes and the magnetization circles the field for ever, at the Larmor frequency. A compass needle would swing to the field; a spin cannot.

$$\frac{d\mathbf m}{dt}=-\gamma\,\mathbf m\times\mathbf H,\qquad E=-\mu_0M_S\,\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf H=-\mu_0M_SH\cos\theta,\qquad f=\frac{\gamma H}{2\pi}$$

Damping drains the energy.

The Gilbert term \(\alpha\,\mathbf m\times d\mathbf m/dt\) opposes the motion. Solving the LLG equation for \(d\mathbf m/dt\) shows what it does: the precession slows by \(1/(1+\alpha^2)\), and a new part of the motion points toward the field. The magnetization spirals in, and its cone closes exponentially.

$$\frac{d\mathbf m}{dt}=-\frac{\gamma}{1+\alpha^2}\Big[\mathbf m\times\mathbf H+\alpha\,\mathbf m\times(\mathbf m\times\mathbf H)\Big],\qquad \tan\frac{\theta}{2}=\tan\frac{\theta_0}{2}\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau=\frac{1+\alpha^2}{\alpha\gamma H}$$

Two wells, and a field to tip them.

A memory bit needs two stable directions. Uniaxial anisotropy provides them: \(E=K_{\rm eff}\sin^2\theta\) has minima at \(\theta=0\) and \(\pi\), up and down the easy axis, with a barrier between. A field up the axis tilts the landscape. The bit starts pointing down, a few degrees off the axis, and flips only when the field is strong enough to remove the down state's well: when \(H\) exceeds the anisotropy field \(H_K\).

$$E=K_{\rm eff}\sin^2\theta-\mu_0M_SH\cos\theta,\qquad \mathbf H_{\rm eff}=\big(H+H_Km_z\big)\hat{\mathbf z},\qquad H_K=\frac{2K_{\rm eff}}{\mu_0M_S},\qquad \Delta E=K_{\rm eff}\Big(1-\frac{H}{H_K}\Big)^2$$

Writing one bit in a crowd.

In a cross-point MRAM, currents in the two lines that cross at the chosen bit make two fields: the word line's along the easy axis, the bit line's along the hard axis. Every other bit on those lines feels only one of them. Stoner and Wohlfarth's astroid says which fields remove the old state: the selected bit must land outside it, the half-selected bits inside. Each field ramps up over 0.5 ns, holds for 2 ns and ramps down.

$$\left|\frac{H_x}{H_K}\right|^{2/3}+\left|\frac{H_e}{H_K}\right|^{2/3}=1\qquad\text{(the astroid: outside it, only one well is left)}$$

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · Precession

        In a field of \(10^5\) A/m (0.126 T), how long does the magnetization take to go once around? And with five times the field, as on the video's slides?

        Show answer

        \(f=\gamma H/2\pi=3.52\) GHz, so one turn takes 284 ps. Five times the field turns it five times faster: 17.6 GHz, 57 ps a turn. The frequency is simply proportional to the field, 28.0 GHz per tesla of \(\mu_0H\), and it does not depend on the cone angle. That is why a magnetic resonance measurement of the frequency is a direct measurement of the field the spins feel.

        Question 2 · Precession

        Start the magnetization at 150° from the field, almost pointing against it, with no damping. Does it swing round toward the field?

        Show answer

        No. It circles the field on a cone of 150°, at the same 3.52 GHz as before, for ever. The torque \(-\gamma\,\mathbf m\times\mathbf H\) is always perpendicular to \(\mathbf H\), so it does no work: the energy stays at \(E=-\mu_0M_SH\cos150°=+0.87\,\mu_0M_SH\), near the top of the landscape, and the ball never rolls. Only damping can bring it down.

        Question 3 · Damping

        With the video's damping, \(\alpha=0.1\), in a field of \(5\times10^5\) A/m and starting 30° from the field: how many turns does the magnetization make before it is within 1° of the field?

        Show answer

        About five and a half: 5.45 turns in 0.31 ns. The cone closes as \(\tan(\theta/2)=\tan(\theta_0/2)e^{-t/\tau}\) with \(\tau=(1+\alpha^2)/\alpha\gamma H=91\) ps, and going from 30° to 1° takes \(\ln(\tan15°/\tan0.5°)=3.4\) of those. Each \(\tau\) lasts \(1/2\pi\alpha=1.6\) turns whatever the field, so a lightly damped magnet makes many turns on its way in.

        Question 4 · Damping

        Which settles fastest: \(\alpha=0.1\), \(\alpha=1\) or \(\alpha=10\)? Surely more damping is always faster?

        Show answer

        \(\alpha=1\): 62 ps from 30° to 1° at \(5\times10^5\) A/m, against 313 ps for both 0.1 and 10. The relaxation time \(\tau\propto(1+\alpha^2)/\alpha\) is smallest at \(\alpha=1\) and the same for \(\alpha\) and \(1/\alpha\). Heavy damping does not speed things up: it slows the whole motion, and the magnetization creeps straight toward the field with almost no precession, like a spoon in honey.

        Question 5 · Switching a bit

        The video's bit: \(K_{\rm eff}=4\times10^5\) J/m³, \(\alpha=0.1\), pointing down. A field of \(10^5\) A/m pushes up the easy axis. Does it switch? What about \(6\times10^5\) A/m?

        Show answer

        Not at \(10^5\) A/m: here \(H_K=2K_{\rm eff}/\mu_0M_S=5\times10^5\) A/m (with \(\mu_0M_S=1.6\) T), so \(h=H/H_K=0.2\), and the down state is still a well, \((1-h)^2=64\%\) as deep as without the field. The magnetization wobbles and settles back down. At \(6\times10^5\) A/m, \(h=1.2\gt1\): the down well is gone and the bit flips up, as in the video.

        Question 6 · Switching a bit

        At \(6\times10^5\) A/m the bit switches. How long does it take to cross the equator from a 5° tilt? What if it starts only 2.5° off the axis?

        Show answer

        0.92 ns from 5°, and 1.23 ns from 2.5°: halving the tilt adds 0.31 ns. Near the down pole the tilt grows exponentially, as \(e^{t/t_0}\) with \(t_0=(1+\alpha^2)/\alpha\gamma(H-H_K)=0.46\) ns, so the delay grows as the logarithm of the starting tilt, and a bit sitting exactly on its axis would never start. Real bits are kicked off the axis by heat, so field switching has a random delay.

        Question 7 · Switching a bit

        Now turn the \(6\times10^5\) A/m field off after 0.8 ns, as the video does once the bit has switched. Does the bit stay switched? What about 1.0 ns?

        Show answer

        After 0.8 ns it falls back down; after 1.0 ns it stays up. With no field the landscape is the symmetric double well, with its barrier at the equator, so all that matters is which side the magnetization is on when the field stops. It crosses at 0.92 ns: at 0.8 ns \(m_z=-0.74\), still in the lower half; at 1.0 ns \(m_z=+0.92\). Once over, the bit keeps its new state with no power, which is what makes MRAM non-volatile.

        Question 8 · Writing MRAM

        Both lines carry enough current for 0.6 \(H_K\). Which bits flip: the selected one, the ones sharing its word line, the ones sharing its bit line?

        Show answer

        Only the selected bit. It feels both fields, and \(0.6^{2/3}+0.6^{2/3}=1.42\gt1\) puts it outside the astroid. Its neighbours feel 0.6 \(H_K\) along one axis only, inside the astroid, so they keep their state. With equal fields the window is from \(2^{-3/2}=0.35\) to 1 \(H_K\). The catch is the current: \(H\approx I/2w\) for a line of width \(w\), so 0.6 \(H_K\) needs 60 mA in a 100 nm line. That is why newer MRAMs switch the bit with a spin current instead (the extra torque \(\mathbf N\) on the video's last slide).

        What is being computed?
        • The magnet: a single domain (a macrospin): one unit vector \(\mathbf m\) for the whole bit, with \(\mu_0M_S=1.6\) T (\(M_S=1.27\times10^6\) A/m, typical of CoFeB; the video does not give a value) and \(\gamma=\mu_0\gamma_e=2.213\times10^5\) m A⁻¹ s⁻¹. With the video's \(K_{\rm eff}=4\times10^5\) J/m³ this gives \(H_K=5\times10^5\) A/m (0.63 T), so its two fields, \(10^5\) and \(6\times10^5\) A/m, fall on either side of it. The energy curves on the video's slides are sketches and are not all drawn to the same scale.
        • The equation: the Gilbert form is rewritten in the explicit Landau–Lifshitz form \(d\mathbf m/dt=-\gamma'[\mathbf m\times\mathbf H_{\rm eff}+\alpha\,\mathbf m\times(\mathbf m\times\mathbf H_{\rm eff})]\), \(\gamma'=\gamma/(1+\alpha^2)\), which is the same equation. It is integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta, with steps of at most 0.04 rad of precession, renormalizing \(|\mathbf m|=1\) after each; steps are split where a field switches off. The two arrows at the tip of \(\mathbf m\) are the two parts of \(d\mathbf m/dt\): around the field and toward it.
        • Fields: \(\mathbf H_{\rm eff}=\mathbf H_{\rm ext}+H_Km_z\hat{\mathbf z}\). Only the applied field and uniaxial anisotropy are included: no demagnetizing field beyond what \(K_{\rm eff}\) absorbs, no exchange (the whole bit turns together) and no thermal noise, so a bit is started a chosen angle off its axis instead.
        • Closed forms: with the field along the easy axis the motion is symmetric about it, and \(dm_z/dt=\alpha\gamma'(H+H_Km_z)(1-m_z^2)\) integrates exactly by partial fractions. The lab uses this for the switching times and to choose how long to run; the relaxation in tab 2 is \(m_z=\tanh(t/\tau+\tanh^{-1}\cos\theta_0)\).
        • Writing MRAM: the bits are independent macrospins (no stray fields between them). The fields ramp linearly over 0.5 ns, hold for 2 ns and ramp down over 0.5 ns. The astroid is the static Stoner–Wohlfarth boundary; the simulation follows it to within about 1% at \(\alpha=0.1\), but near its edge a bit can take longer than the pulse to escape, and with little damping it can overshoot and switch just inside it. Line currents use \(H\approx I/2w\), the field next to a wide flat strip.
        • Checks: the lab's integrator was checked against SciPy's DOP853 solver for precession, damping, switching, pulses and the write ramps, to better than \(10^{-7}\), and the switching-time formula against direct numerical integration. The notebook repeats the calculation independently and asserts every number quoted in the answers.
