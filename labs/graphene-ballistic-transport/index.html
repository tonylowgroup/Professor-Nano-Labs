One channel, two leads, three Dirac points

A graphene channel of length \(L\) sits between semi-infinite graphene source and drain leads. Each region has its own Dirac-point energy, \(E_{D,S}\), \(E_{D,C}\) and \(E_{D,D}\). The sheet is wide, so the transverse momentum \(k_y\) is conserved and each \(k_y\) becomes its own one-dimensional chain, with a projected gap \(2\hbar v_F|k_y|\) near the Dirac point. The channel is ballistic and phase coherent, and the lab solves it with the non-equilibrium Green's function (NEGF) method:

$$G^r(E,k_y)=\big[(E+i\eta)I-H(k_y)-\Sigma_S-\Sigma_D\big]^{-1},\qquad T=\Gamma_S\Gamma_D\,|G^r_{1N}|^2,\qquad \rho_i=-\tfrac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^r_{ii}$$

The three Dirac points are electron energies in eV, not applied voltages or chemical potentials. Transmission is per spin and per valley mode; the LDOS is available spectral weight, not occupied charge.

      Step 2 · Predict, then test

      Predict first

      Load each example, make a prediction, then check it against the lab.

      Question 1

      All three Dirac points sit at −0.20 eV and ky = 0.18 nm⁻¹. At which energies does an electron get through, and does the channel length matter?

      Show answer

      Wherever the lead mode is open, T is essentially 1 (1.000000 at E = 0) and the length makes no difference: with no interfaces there is nothing to reflect from. Between about −0.30 and −0.10 eV T is exactly 0, because this ky has a projected gap of 0.21 eV around the Dirac point and no propagating state exists in either lead.

      Question 2

      At E₀ = 0 the electron must cross from n-type graphene into a p-type channel and back. Will it get through better at normal incidence (ky = 0) or at an angle (ky = 0.18 nm⁻¹)?

      Show answer

      At normal incidence, almost perfectly: T = 0.995. This is Klein tunnelling: graphene's pseudospin forbids head-on backscattering, so a potential step that would stop an ordinary electron barely reflects it. At ky = 0.18 nm⁻¹ T drops to 0.765, and to 0.22 at 0.24 nm⁻¹. Oblique electrons do reflect, and their transmission oscillates with energy.

      Question 3

      Look at the transmission peaks for the selected ky = 0.18 nm⁻¹ near E = 0. If you double the channel length to 80 nm, what happens to their spacing?

      Show answer

      The spacing roughly halves, from about 37 meV to about 19 meV near E = 0. The peaks are Fabry–Pérot resonances: waves bouncing between the two interfaces add up when a whole number of half-wavelengths fits in the channel. Their spacing is roughly \(\pi\hbar|v_x|/L\), so a longer channel packs them closer together.

      Question 4

      The probe sits at the channel's Dirac point, where this ky mode is evanescent in the channel. T is about 0.03 for ky = 0.06 nm⁻¹ and L = 40 nm. Which hurts more: doubling ky or doubling L?

      Show answer

      Both, by the same amount: either change drops T to about 2 × 10⁻⁴, over 100 times smaller. Inside the channel the wave decays as \(e^{-|k_y|x}\), so tunnelling depends on \(|k_y|L\) and approaches \(T\approx\mathrm{sech}^2(|k_y|L)\) for heavily doped leads (0.032 here). Look at the LDOS: the leads' tails reach into the channel from both sides.

      Question 5

      Now the leads are at 0 eV and the channel at −0.20 eV, with ky = 0.24 nm⁻¹. Find bright horizontal stripes in the channel's LDOS between −0.14 and +0.14 eV. Do they carry current?

      Show answer

      No. In that energy range neither lead has a propagating state for this ky (their mode gap is ±0.14 eV), so T is exactly 0, even at E ≈ 0.014 eV where the channel LDOS is strongest. These are states trapped in the channel. A large local density of states is not enough for current; the states must also connect to propagating modes in both leads.

      Question 6

      This is the n–p–n device with all three Dirac points and the probe energy shifted up by 0.05 eV. What should T be at the probe energy, compared with 0.765 in Question 2?

      Show answer

      Exactly the same: 0.765. Only energy differences matter, so shifting every Dirac point and the probe energy together just slides all the curves along the energy axis. It is the offsets between regions, not their absolute values, that create the junctions.

      The companion Jupyter notebook derives the mode-space Hamiltonian and lead self-energies, solves NEGF with a recursive Green's function, plots every view in this lab, and runs numerical checks against a direct matrix inverse and the continuum Dirac barrier. It needs only NumPy and Matplotlib.

      What is being computed?
      • Hamiltonian: graphene's nearest-neighbour pz tight-binding model in mode space. For each conserved \(k_y\) (measured from the chosen valley), the two-orbital cells unfold into a chain with alternating hoppings \(-t, b, -t, b,\dots\), where \(b = 2t\cos(\nu\pi/3 + a_0k_y/2)\), \(t = 2.7\) eV and \(a_{cc} = 0.142\) nm, giving \(v_F \approx 0.874\times10^6\) m/s.
      • Leads: exact retarded self-energies of semi-infinite uniform graphene leads, from the root of \(z\Sigma^2-(z^2+b^2-t^2)\Sigma+zb^2=0\) with \(\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma\le0\). No wide-band approximation.
      • Solver: a recursive Green's function (two passes along the chain) gives \(G^r_{1N}\) for the Caroli transmission and the full diagonal \(G^r_{ii}\) for the LDOS. If either lead mode is closed, T is set to exactly 0; an evanescent channel is kept, so tunnelling is exact.
      • Grids: 451 energies from −0.45 to 0.45 eV (2 meV steps) for the curves and the map. The LDOS map's fixed colour range is 10⁻³ to 5 states/(eV·nm) on the log scale, or 0 to 5 on the linear scale; values above 5 saturate in the picture but are kept in the CSV.
      • Sweep: the Sweep button moves only the channel Dirac point, from −0.35 to +0.35 eV in 5 meV steps, and recalculates every plot at each step. The leads, the length and E₀ stay fixed.
      • Not in the model: self-consistent electrostatics, disorder, phonons, edge roughness and a bias between the reservoirs. The finite-ky gap is a projected mode gap, not a gap in the 2D sheet.
      • Checks: this page runs the notebook's own JavaScript engine, which matches the notebook's Python solver to about 10⁻¹⁴.

      Primary method: T. Low, S. Hong, J. Appenzeller, S. Datta and M. Lundstrom, Conductance asymmetry of graphene pn junction, IEEE Trans. Electron Devices 56, 1292 (2009), arXiv:0811.1295. This lab keeps the coherent finite channel, unlike that paper's single-junction conductance calculation. Related continuum results: Katsnelson, Novoselov and Geim (2006); Tworzydło et al. (2006).
