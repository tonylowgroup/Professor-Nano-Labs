The Hydrogen Atom

One electron bound to one proton is the only atom whose Schrödinger equation can be solved exactly. Choose the three quantum numbers and see the orbital in 3D, cut it open to find its nodes, watch the centrifugal barrier push the electron away from the nucleus, and trace each line of hydrogen's spectrum back to a jump between two energy levels.

One electron, one proton

The electron feels the Coulomb attraction of the proton, \(V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r\). Because \(V\) depends only on the distance \(r\), the wavefunction separates into a radial part and a spherical harmonic, and three whole numbers label every solution:

$$\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\varphi)=R_{nl}(r)\,Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi),\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots,\quad l=0,1,\dots,n-1,\quad m=-l,\dots,l$$
$$R_{nl}(r)=\sqrt{\left(\frac{2}{na}\right)^3\frac{(n-l-1)!}{2n\,(n+l)!}}\;e^{-r/na}\left(\frac{2r}{na}\right)^lL_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}\!\left(\frac{2r}{na}\right),\qquad E_n=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_ea^2}\frac{1}{n^2}=-\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}$$

Here \(a=4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/m_ee^2=0.529\) Å is the Bohr radius and \(L_q^p\) are the associated Laguerre polynomials. The energy depends on \(n\) alone, so each shell holds \(n^2\) orbitals with the same energy. As in the video, the proton is held still.

Three numbers fix an orbital.

\(n\) sets the energy and the size, \(l\) the shape (s, p, d, f, g for \(l=0,1,2,3,4\)), and \(m\) the orientation. An orbital has \(n-1\) nodes, surfaces where \(\psi=0\): \(n-l-1\) of them are spheres from the radial part, and \(l\) are planes or cones from the angular part. Real orbitals, the ones chemists draw, are red where \(\psi\gt0\) and pale where \(\psi\lt0\). The complex ones, \(Y_l^m\propto e^{im\varphi}\), are coloured by their phase.

$$\tilde Y_l^{m}\propto\begin{cases}\cos(m\varphi)\,P_l^{m}(\cos\theta)&m\gt0\\ P_l^0(\cos\theta)&m=0\\ \sin(|m|\varphi)\,P_l^{|m|}(\cos\theta)&m\lt0\end{cases}\qquad\text{e.g. } p_x=\frac{\psi_{n1,-1}-\psi_{n1,1}}{\sqrt2}$$

A one-dimensional problem with a centrifugal barrier.

With \(u(r)=rR(r)\), the radial equation is an ordinary Schrödinger equation in one dimension, in an effective potential: the Coulomb well plus a centrifugal barrier \(\hbar^2l(l+1)/2m_er^2\) that keeps electrons with angular momentum away from the nucleus. The chance of finding the electron between \(r\) and \(r+dr\) is \(P(r)\,dr\), with \(P=r^2R^2=u^2\).

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2}+\left[-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}+\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}\right]u=E\,u,\qquad \int_0^\infty P(r)\,dr=1$$

Every line is a jump between two levels.

When the electron drops from level \(n_i\) to \(n_f\), it emits a photon that carries away the energy difference. The Bohr energies give the Rydberg formula. Lines ending on the same level form a series: Lyman (\(n_f=1\)) in the ultraviolet, Balmer (\(n_f=2\)) with four lines in the visible, then Paschen and Brackett in the infrared.

$$\frac{hc}{\lambda}=E_{n_i}-E_{n_f}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac1\lambda=R\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2}-\frac{1}{n_i^2}\right),\qquad R=\frac{m_ee^4}{8c\,\varepsilon_0^2h^3}=1.097\times10^7\ \text{m}^{-1}$$

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · Orbitals

        The 2s orbital is spherical, like 1s. Where is its node, and what is on either side of it?

        Show answer

        A sphere of radius \(2a=1.06\) Å, where \(R_{20}\propto(1-r/2a)\) changes sign. Inside it the wavefunction is positive (red), outside negative (pale). The electron is never found exactly on the node, yet it is found on both sides of it: a node is not a wall.

        Question 2 · Orbitals

        How many nodes does \(3d_{z^2}\) have, and what shape are they?

        Show answer

        Two, as for every \(n=3\) orbital (\(n-1=2\)). Both are angular, since \(n-l-1=0\): \(3\cos^2\theta-1=0\) on two cones at \(\theta=54.7^\circ\) and \(125.3^\circ\). They separate the two red lobes along \(z\) from the pale ring around the middle. Not every angular node is a plane.

        Question 3 · Orbitals

        The complex orbital \(\psi_{2,1,+1}\propto\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi}\). What shape is \(|\psi|^2\)? Is it the \(2p_x\) orbital?

        Show answer

        A doughnut around the \(z\) axis, the same for \(m=-1\): \(|e^{\pm i\varphi}|=1\), so nothing depends on \(\varphi\). Its phase winds once around \(z\), which is what gives it angular momentum \(L_z=+\hbar\). The two-lobed \(2p_x\) is a different state, the superposition \((\psi_{2,1,-1}-\psi_{2,1,1})/\sqrt2\), with the same energy but no definite \(L_z\). Switch between Real and Complex to compare.

        Question 4 · Orbitals

        In the ground state, where is the electron most likely to be: at the nucleus, or somewhere else?

        Show answer

        Both answers are right, for different questions. The probability density \(|\psi|^2\propto e^{-2r/a}\) is largest at the nucleus. But the most likely distance is \(r=a\): a shell of radius \(r\) has volume \(4\pi r^2dr\), and \(P(r)=4r^2e^{-2r/a}/a^3\) peaks at \(r=a\), Bohr's radius. The average distance is \(1.5a\).

        Question 5 · Radial equation

        A classical electron with the 1s energy, \(-13.6\) eV, could never get further than \(r=2a\), where \(V(r)=E\). How much of the time is the quantum electron beyond that point?

        Show answer

        About 24% of the time: \(\int_{2a}^\infty P\,dr=13e^{-4}=0.238\). The wavefunction leaks into the classically forbidden region, where it decays exponentially rather than stopping. This is the same physics as tunnelling.

        Question 6 · Radial equation

        Of 3s, 3p and 3d, which gets closest to the nucleus? Which is smallest on average?

        Show answer

        Only 3s reaches the nucleus: near \(r=0\), \(P\propto r^{2l+2}\), so the centrifugal barrier keeps 3p and 3d out. Yet on average 3d is the most compact: \(\langle r\rangle=\tfrac12[3n^2-l(l+1)]a\) gives 13.5\(a\), 12.5\(a\) and 10.5\(a\). The 3s spends some time near the nucleus but has a long tail; 3d has no nodes and sits at \(r\approx9a\).

        Question 7 · Spectral lines

        Hydrogen glows red. Which jump makes that red line, and what is its wavelength?

        Show answer

        \(n=3\to2\), the first Balmer line, H\(\alpha\): \(1/\lambda=R(1/4-1/9)=5R/36\), so \(\lambda=656.1\) nm. The measured line is at 656.5 nm in vacuum (656.3 nm in air); most of the 0.05% difference comes from the proton recoiling, which the video's model leaves out.

        Question 8 · Spectral lines

        How short can a Lyman wavelength be? And what happens to a photon with even more energy?

        Show answer

        The lines crowd towards the series limit, \(n_i\to\infty\): \(\lambda=1/R=91.1\) nm, a photon of 13.6 eV, exactly the ionisation energy. A photon with more energy than that cannot land on a bound level. It is absorbed by knocking the electron free, and the spare energy goes into the electron's motion, giving a continuum instead of a line.

        Run the same calculation in Python

        The companion Jupyter notebook builds \(R_{nl}\) from the Laguerre polynomials and checks it against the video's formulas, verifies normalisation and orthogonality, plots radial functions and probability slices of the s, p and d orbitals, solves the radial equation numerically to recover the Bohr energies, and computes the spectral series. It needs NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib.

        Download notebook .ipynb · 15 KB
        What is being computed?
        • Wavefunctions: \(R_{nl}\) exactly as in the video, from the associated Laguerre polynomials, and \(Y_l^m\) with the Condon–Shortley phase. The real orbitals are \(\sqrt2(-1)^m\,\mathrm{Re}\,Y_l^m\) for \(m\gt0\) and \(\sqrt2(-1)^m\,\mathrm{Im}\,Y_l^{|m|}\) for \(m\lt0\), labelled as in the video's table of orbitals: \(m=1\) is \(p_x\) and \(d_{xz}\), \(m=-1\) is \(p_y\) and \(d_{yz}\). All are evaluated in your browser.
        • Surface: \(|\psi|^2\) is computed on a grid of 60³ to 92³ points around the atom. The surface is the level \(|\psi|^2=c\) where the region of higher density holds the chosen share of the probability. It is a choice of display, not the edge of the electron. Real orbitals get two surfaces, \(\psi=\pm\sqrt c\); complex ones get \(|\psi|^2=c\), coloured by the phase of \(\psi\).
        • Cloud: 20,000 positions drawn at random from \(|\psi|^2\): the distance from \(P(r)\), the direction from \(|Y|^2\). Where the cloud is dense the electron is likely to be found.
        • Slice: \(|\psi|^2\) or \(\psi\) on the plane \(y=0\) or \(z=0\). The brightness is \((|\psi|^2/|\psi|^2_{\max})^{0.4}\), to bring out the fainter outer lobes; it is not a projection through the atom.
        • Radial quantities: nodes are the zeros of \(L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}\); \(\langle r\rangle=\tfrac12[3n^2-l(l+1)]a\); the turning points solve \(V_\text{eff}(r)=E_n\); the probabilities are integrated numerically.
        • Spectrum: the Bohr energies with \(13.606\) eV and the Rydberg constant \(R_\infty=1.0974\times10^7\) m⁻¹, with the proton held still as in the video. A moving proton (the reduced mass) shifts every line by 0.05%. Wavelengths are in vacuum. The level diagram places level \(n\) at a height proportional to \(1/n\), so that the upper levels do not overlap; the energies are written beside them.
        • Checks: every \(R_{nl}\), \(Y_l^m\), node position and probability agrees with SciPy to about \(10^{-13}\) or better; see the notebook.
