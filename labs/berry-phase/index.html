Berry Phase, Connection and Curvature

Take a quantum state around a closed loop and it comes back with a phase that remembers the shape of the loop. Carry a spin slowly around a cone and measure that phase, build it from a chain of states in any gauge you like, then move into the Brillouin zone of two crystals, the Qi–Wu–Zhang model and gapped or Haldane graphene, and map the Berry curvature whose total is the quantized Chern number.

A phase that remembers the path

Hold an arrow flat against a globe and slide it around a closed loop, never letting it twist. When you get back to the start, it points a different way: it has turned by the area the loop encloses on the sphere, its solid angle. A Foucault pendulum shows the same effect: the Earth carries it once a day around its circle of latitude, and its swing turns by 2π × sin(latitude), a full turn at a pole and none at the equator.

Quantum states do this with their phase. Change a system slowly around a loop and its state comes back with an extra phase that depends only on the shape of the loop, not on how long the trip took: the Berry phase. The lab builds the idea in three steps: a spin in a turning magnetic field, a chain of states as a computer sees it, and electrons in a crystal.

Phase
The angle of a complex number. A state's overall phase can't be seen on its own, but differences in phase show up when waves interfere.
Solid angle
The area a loop encloses on a sphere of radius 1. The whole sphere is 4π; half of it, 2π.
Berry phase
The extra phase a state picks up when it is taken slowly around a closed loop. It is set by the loop's geometry alone.
Gauge
The free choice of each state's overall phase. Nothing you can measure depends on it.
Berry curvature
Berry phase per unit area: the Berry phase of a small loop, divided by the area it encloses.
Chern number
The total Berry curvature of a band, divided by 2π. It is always a whole number.
The math

Change the parameters \(\mathbf R\) of a Hamiltonian slowly and a state stays in its eigenstate \(|n,\mathbf R\rangle\), collecting the familiar dynamical phase. Berry noticed that it collects a second, geometric phase \(\gamma_n\), the integral of the Berry connection \(\mathbf A_n\) along the path. Around a closed loop, Stokes's theorem turns it into the flux of the Berry curvature \(\Omega_n\) through the loop:

$$|\Psi(t)\rangle=e^{i\gamma_n(t)}\,e^{-\frac i\hbar\int_0^tE_n\,dt'}\,|n,\mathbf R(t)\rangle,\qquad \mathbf A_n=i\langle n,\mathbf R|\nabla_{\mathbf R}|n,\mathbf R\rangle,\qquad \gamma_n=\oint_C\mathbf A_n\cdot d\mathbf R=\iint_S\Omega_n\,dS,\qquad \Omega_n=\nabla_{\mathbf R}\times\mathbf A_n$$

The connection depends on the arbitrary phase of each \(|n,\mathbf R\rangle\), the gauge; the phase around a closed loop and the curvature do not, which is why they can be measured. Here the parameters are first the direction of a magnetic field acting on a spin, then the wavevector \(\mathbf k\) of an electron in a crystal.

Show

Carry a spin slowly around a loop and it comes back with an extra phase.

A spin in a magnetic field lines up with the field. Turn the field slowly around a cone and the spin follows it round. When the field is back where it started, so is the spin, but its quantum state has picked up two phases: an ordinary one that grows with time, and the Berry phase, which depends only on the loop. It is minus half the loop's solid angle, \(-\mathcal S/2\).

What to notice: the green arrow, carried round without turning, comes back turned by the solid angle. The spin's Berry phase is half of that, the other way. Make the loop slower and the measured phase settles on it, however slow you go.

The math

A spin-½ in a field along \(\mathbf n\), \(H=-\tfrac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), sits in the lower level \(|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\), spin along the field. Turn the field around a cone of half-angle \(\theta\) and, if you go slowly compared with the Larmor period \(2\pi/\omega_0\), the spin follows. Back at the start, the state is the one it began in, times a phase: the dynamical phase \(\omega_0T/2\), and the Berry phase, which does not care how long the trip took, only about the loop: minus half the solid angle \(\mathcal S\) it encloses.

$$\gamma=\oint i\langle{+}\mathbf n|\nabla_{\mathbf n}{+}\mathbf n\rangle\cdot d\mathbf n=-\frac{\mathcal S}{2}=-\pi\,(1-\cos\theta),\qquad |{+}\mathbf n\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac\theta2\\e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac\theta2\end{pmatrix}$$

A computer sees a loop as a chain of states, and you choose each one's phase.

A computer cannot follow a smooth loop, so it takes a chain of states around it. Each state's overall phase is a free choice, a gauge, like setting each clock in a row to any time you like. The phase from one state to the next depends on those choices; the total around the closed chain does not, because each state's choice enters once on the way in and once on the way out, and cancels. That total is the Berry phase.

What to notice: press Random, then New random phases: every link changes, the total does not. With parallel transport, the video's choice, all of the phase moves into the last link.

The math

On a computer a loop is a chain of states \(|u_1\rangle,\dots,|u_N\rangle\), and each comes with a phase of your choosing: a gauge. Each overlap \(\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle\) depends on it; but around a closed chain every state appears once as a ket and once as a bra, so the phases cancel in the product. Minus its phase is the Berry phase, and for neighbouring states \(\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle\approx1-i\mathbf A\cdot\Delta\mathbf k\): the video's parallel transport, which as the points close up becomes \(\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf k\).

$$\gamma_N=-\arg\prod_{j=1}^{N}\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle=-\arg\mathrm{Tr}\big[P_1P_2\cdots P_N\big],\qquad P_j=|u_j\rangle\langle u_j|,\quad |u_{N+1}\rangle=|u_1\rangle$$

In a crystal, the loop is in momentum space.

An electron in a crystal has a wavevector \(\mathbf k\) where the spin had a field direction, and at each \(\mathbf k\) its state points somewhere on a sphere, just as the spin did. Take \(\mathbf k\) around a loop and the electron picks up a Berry phase. The Berry phase per unit area is the Berry curvature, mapped here over graphene's Brillouin zone. Added up over the whole zone it gives 2π times a whole number, the Chern number, which counts how many times the electron's states wrap the sphere.

What to notice: graphene on a substrate has curvature of opposite signs in its two valleys, K and K′, so they cancel and C = 0. Haldane's graphene gives them the same sign, and C = 1: a quantized Hall effect with no magnetic field.

The math

For a band in a crystal, the cell-periodic Bloch state \(|u_{\mathbf k}\rangle\) plays the part of the spin state and the wavevector \(\mathbf k\) that of the field. In a two-band model, \(H(\mathbf k)=d_0+\mathbf d(\mathbf k)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), each \(\mathbf k\) gives a direction \(\mathbf n=\mathbf d/|\mathbf d|\), and the lower band is the spin state pointing against it. So the Berry curvature is half the solid angle that \(\mathbf n\) sweeps per unit area of \(\mathbf k\), and its total over the zone, \(2\pi\) for each time \(\mathbf n\) wraps the sphere, gives the Chern number, which quantizes the Hall conductance of the filled band.

$$\Omega(\mathbf k)=\partial_{k_x}A_y-\partial_{k_y}A_x=\tfrac12\,\mathbf n\cdot\big(\partial_{k_x}\mathbf n\times\partial_{k_y}\mathbf n\big),\qquad C=\frac1{2\pi}\iint_{\rm BZ}\Omega\,d^2k\in\mathbb Z,\qquad |\sigma_{xy}|=|C|\,\frac{e^2}{h}$$

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · A spin's loop

        The field sweeps once around the equator, slowly. The spin follows it and ends where it began. What Berry phase has it picked up?

        Show answer

        Minus π, half a turn: the state comes back with its sign flipped, \(-|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\). The equator encloses half the sphere, a solid angle of \(2\pi\), and the Berry phase is minus half of that. The lab measures \(-0.995\pi\); the last half per cent comes from going round in a finite time. The green arrow, meanwhile, has turned a full 360° and looks unchanged: a spin's state needs two turns, not one, to come back to itself. The sign flip is real: interfere a neutron whose spin has been carried around with one whose spin has not, and it shows.

        Question 2 · A spin's loop

        A 60° cone, whose Berry phase is \(-\pi/2\), a quarter turn. Now go around it fast: in a single turn of the spin's own wobble. What phase do you measure? How much slower must you go to get within 1% of \(-\pi/2\)?

        Show answer

        Only \(-0.245\pi\), about half the Berry phase: the spin cannot keep up, it wobbles around the field, and at one point it has only a 75% chance of pointing along it. Slow down and the phase closes in: \(-0.464\pi\) at 10 turns of the wobble, \(-0.496\pi\) at 100, so about 75 for 1%. The error falls like 1 over the loop time; the chance of the spin leaving the field falls much faster, like 1 over its square, so a spin can look as if it follows long before its phase is right.

        Question 3 · Gauge

        Give every state in the chain a random phase, and press "New random phases" a few times. Which numbers change, and which do not?

        Show answer

        Every link changes, and so does the phase collected from the first state to the last without closing the loop: these depend on your choice of phases and cannot be measured. The total around the closed chain, \(\gamma_N\), does not change at all, because each state's random phase enters once on the way in and once on the way out. The little hands, which show each state's phase, point every which way.

        Question 4 · Crystals

        Graphene on a substrate, such as boron nitride, which makes its two carbon sites different and opens a gap (\(M=0.2\,t\)). The loop around the valley K picks up \(-0.81\pi\). What does a loop around the other valley, K′, pick up? What is the Chern number?

        Show answer

        \(+0.81\pi\), and \(C=0\). Each valley holds a peak of Berry curvature, but the two have opposite signs, as they must in a crystal without magnetism: running time backwards swaps K and K′ and flips the curvature. They cancel, so there is no Hall effect; but electrons in valleys K and K′ are pushed sideways in opposite directions, the valley Hall effect. Choose "Plain graphene" and each valley's curvature shrinks into a single point, while a loop around it still picks up exactly π.

        Question 5 · Crystals

        Haldane's graphene: no substrate, but an extra hopping between next-nearest neighbours that carries a phase, which breaks time-reversal symmetry with no overall magnetic field. What is \(C\)? Now raise the substrate's mass \(M\): at what value does \(C\) change?

        Show answer

        \(C=1\): the two valleys' curvature now has the same sign, and the electron's states wrap the sphere once. \(C\) stays 1 as \(M\) rises, until \(M=0.52\,t\) (that is \(3\sqrt3\,t_2\sin\varphi\)): there the gap closes at K, and above it \(C=0\). A whole number cannot change gradually, so the gap has to close for it to change. That is also why a Chern insulator must conduct along its edges, where it meets the vacuum, which has \(C=0\). The Qi–Wu–Zhang model, under "Go deeper", does the same.

        Question 6 · ChainsChallenge

        Only three states, 120° apart, on the equator. How far is the chain's phase \(\gamma_3\) from the exact \(-\pi\)? And with three states on the 60° circle?

        Show answer

        On the equator, not at all: \(\gamma_3=-\pi\) exactly. The chain's phase is exact, not for the smooth circle, but for the polygon joining the states by great circles: it is minus half that polygon's solid angle. On the equator the great circles are the equator itself. On the 60° circle the triangle cuts the corners, \(\gamma_3=-0.318\pi\) against \(-\pi/2\), and the error falls like \(1/N^2\): \(-0.480\pi\) with 8 states, \(-0.499\pi\) with 30.

        Question 7 · GaugeChallenge

        Now choose each state's phase so that the step from the previous state has no phase at all: the video's parallel transport. Where has the Berry phase gone?

        Show answer

        Into the last link. Every other step now has zero phase, each hand is carried along without turning, and when the chain closes, the last state does not match the first: the whole \(\gamma_N\) sits in that one link. You can move the Berry phase from link to link, but never make it go away. Back at the first state the hand has turned by the polygon's solid angle, \(-2\gamma_N\): it is the same arrow as the green one in tab 1, carried around without turning.

        Question 8 · A spin's loopChallenge

        Open the cone past the equator, to 120°. The loop now encloses three quarters of the sphere, \(\mathcal S=3\pi\), so is the Berry phase \(-3\pi/2\)? But on its other side the loop also encloses the southern cap, \(\mathcal S=\pi\). Which is right?

        Show answer

        Both: \(-3\pi/2\) and \(+\pi/2\) (the southern cap is traversed the other way round) differ by exactly \(2\pi\), a full turn, so they are the same phase. The lab, which follows the phase continuously around the loop, measures \(-1.496\pi\), the same phase as \(+0.504\pi\). It works only because the two choices of "inside" differ by \(4\pi\), the whole sphere, and the spin's phase is half of that. A spin's Berry curvature is that of a magnetic monopole at the centre of the sphere, and this is Dirac's argument for why the charge of a monopole must be quantized.

        What is being computed?
        • The spin's loop: \(H(t)=-\tfrac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}\mathbf n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), with \(\mathbf n\) turning about \(z\) at a constant rate \(\omega=2\pi/T\) on a cone of half-angle \(\theta\). In the frame turning with the field the Hamiltonian is constant, \(H'=H(0)-\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z\), so the evolution is exact: \(|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-i\omega t\sigma_z/2}e^{-iH't/\hbar}|{+}\mathbf n(0)\rangle\), with no time steps. The measured phase is \(\arg\langle{+}\mathbf n(t)|\psi(t)\rangle-\omega_0t/2\), with \(|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\) in the gauge written above, followed continuously in time; it becomes the Berry phase when the trip is slow, and the probability \(|\langle{+}\mathbf n|\psi\rangle|^2\) shows how well the spin is following.
        • The green arrow: a tangent vector carried along the loop without turning about \(\mathbf n\) (parallel transport on the sphere). Relative to the local direction of decreasing latitude it turns by \(-\cos\theta\) per radian of \(\varphi\), and after a full loop it has turned by the enclosed solid angle \(\mathcal S\) (mod \(2\pi\)), twice the spin's phase and opposite in sign.
        • Chains of states: \(\gamma_N=-\arg\prod\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle\), computed in complex arithmetic, and \(-\arg\mathrm{Tr}[P_1\cdots P_N]\) from \(2\times2\) projectors. The smooth gauge is \(|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\) as written in tab 1; the random gauge multiplies each state by a random \(e^{i\chi_j}\); the parallel-transport gauge fixes each phase so that \(\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle\gt0\) for \(j\lt N\). Each flag is the real part of \(\psi^{T}(i\sigma_y)\boldsymbol\sigma\psi\), a unit vector at right angles to \(\mathbf n\) that turns by \(2\chi\) when the state's phase changes by \(\chi\). The orange slice runs from the north pole down the meridian \(\varphi=0\), along the equator to \(\varphi=\Delta\varphi\) and back up, enclosing \(\mathcal S=\Delta\varphi\).
        • The crystals: Qi–Wu–Zhang, \(\mathbf d=(\sin k_x,\ \sin k_y,\ m-\cos k_x-\cos k_y)\), lattice constant 1. The honeycomb: graphene's nearest-neighbour hopping \(t=1\) (carbon–carbon distance 1), \(d_x+id_y=\sum_j e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j}\), with the Bloch sums built on the atoms' actual positions (so \(H(\mathbf k)\) is not periodic in the reciprocal lattice; another choice of basis would change the local curvature map, but not the Chern number or the phase of a loop centred on K or K′), a sublattice mass \(M\sigma_z\), and Haldane's next-nearest-neighbour hopping \(t_2e^{\pm i\varphi}\), which gives \(d_0=2t_2\cos\varphi\sum_i\cos\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf b_i\) and adds \(-2t_2\sin\varphi\sum_i\sin\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf b_i\) to \(d_z\). The masses at the Dirac points are \(M\mp3\sqrt3\,t_2\sin\varphi\) at K and K′.
        • Curvature, connection and Chern number: \(\Omega=\mathbf d\cdot(\partial_{k_x}\mathbf d\times\partial_{k_y}\mathbf d)/2|\mathbf d|^3\) for the lower band, from analytic derivatives. The connection is drawn in two gauges: N, \(|u\rangle=(-e^{-i\phi}\sin\frac\vartheta2,\ \cos\frac\vartheta2)\) with \(\mathbf A_N=\sin^2\frac\vartheta2\,\nabla\phi\), smooth except where \(\mathbf n=-\hat z\); and S, \(\mathbf A_S=\mathbf A_N-\nabla\phi\), smooth except where \(\mathbf n=+\hat z\) (\(\vartheta,\phi\) are the angles of \(\mathbf n\)). The crossed circles mark the vortices, where the gauge fails. The Chern number uses Fukui, Hatsugai and Suzuki's method on a 48 × 48 grid over the unit cell of the reciprocal lattice: the sum of the Berry phases of the small plaquettes, which is an integer by construction. The loop's Berry phase uses 400 states on the circle, doubled until the result settles (with Richardson extrapolation); the flux of \(\Omega\) through it and \(\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf k\) in the chosen gauge use adaptive Gauss–Kronrod quadrature, which resolves the narrow peaks of curvature near a closing gap. The colour scale of the map follows the bulk of the curvature, so the narrowest peaks saturate.
        • Conventions: \(\mathbf A=i\langle u|\nabla u\rangle\), as in the video, so a state picks up \(e^{+i\gamma}\) with \(\gamma=\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf k\), and \(C=\frac1{2\pi}\int\Omega\). With these, the Qi–Wu–Zhang lower band has \(C=-1\) for \(0\lt m\lt2\) and \(+1\) for \(-2\lt m\lt0\); other sources may flip either sign. The sign of the Hall conductance depends further on the sign of the charge and the orientation of the axes, so only \(|\sigma_{xy}|\) is quoted.
        • Checks: the chains' phases agree with \(-\tfrac12\) the solid angles of their great-circle polygons to \(10^{-12}\), and approach \(-\mathcal S/2\) like \(1/N^2\); the adiabatic phase converges to \(-\mathcal S/2\) like \(1/T\); a loop's Berry phase agrees with the flux of \(\Omega\) through it (Stokes), mod \(2\pi\), to about \(10^{-6}\) or better, also next to a closing gap; the Chern numbers agree with \(\frac1{2\pi}\int\Omega\) and with the masses at the Dirac points.
