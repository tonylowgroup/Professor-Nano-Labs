Berry Phase, Connection and Curvature

Take a quantum state around a closed loop and it comes back with a phase that remembers the shape of the loop. Carry a spin slowly around a cone and measure that phase, build it from a chain of states in any gauge you like, then move into the Brillouin zone of two crystals, the Qi–Wu–Zhang model and gapped or Haldane graphene, and map the Berry curvature whose total is the quantized Chern number.

A phase that remembers the path

Change the parameters \(\mathbf R\) of a Hamiltonian slowly and a state stays in its eigenstate \(|n,\mathbf R\rangle\), collecting the familiar dynamical phase. Berry noticed that it collects a second, geometric phase \(\gamma_n\), the integral of the Berry connection \(\mathbf A_n\) along the path. Around a closed loop, Stokes's theorem turns it into the flux of the Berry curvature \(\Omega_n\) through the loop:

$$|\Psi(t)\rangle=e^{i\gamma_n(t)}\,e^{-\frac i\hbar\int_0^tE_n\,dt'}\,|n,\mathbf R(t)\rangle,\qquad \mathbf A_n=i\langle n,\mathbf R|\nabla_{\mathbf R}|n,\mathbf R\rangle,\qquad \gamma_n=\oint_C\mathbf A_n\cdot d\mathbf R=\iint_S\Omega_n\,dS,\qquad \Omega_n=\nabla_{\mathbf R}\times\mathbf A_n$$

The connection depends on the arbitrary phase of each \(|n,\mathbf R\rangle\), the gauge; the phase around a closed loop and the curvature do not, which is why they can be measured. Here the parameters are first the direction of a magnetic field acting on a spin, then the wavevector \(\mathbf k\) of an electron in a crystal.

Carry a spin slowly around a loop and it comes back with an extra phase.

A spin-½ in a field along \(\mathbf n\), \(H=-\tfrac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), sits in the lower level \(|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\), spin along the field. Turn the field around a cone of half-angle \(\theta\) and, if you go slowly compared with the Larmor period \(2\pi/\omega_0\), the spin follows. Back at the start, the state is the one it began in, times a phase: the dynamical phase \(\omega_0T/2\), and the Berry phase, which does not care how long the trip took, only about the loop: minus half the solid angle \(\mathcal S\) it encloses.

$$\gamma=\oint i\langle{+}\mathbf n|\nabla_{\mathbf n}{+}\mathbf n\rangle\cdot d\mathbf n=-\frac{\mathcal S}{2}=-\pi\,(1-\cos\theta),\qquad |{+}\mathbf n\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac\theta2\\e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac\theta2\end{pmatrix}$$

A loop as a chain of states: only the closed product means anything.

On a computer a loop is a chain of states \(|u_1\rangle,\dots,|u_N\rangle\), and each comes with a phase of your choosing: a gauge. Each overlap \(\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle\) depends on it; but around a closed chain every state appears once as a ket and once as a bra, so the phases cancel in the product. Its phase is the Berry phase, and for neighbouring states \(\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle\approx1-i\mathbf A\cdot\Delta\mathbf k\): the video's parallel transport, which as the points close up becomes \(\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf k\).

$$\gamma_N=-\arg\prod_{j=1}^{N}\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle=-\arg\mathrm{Tr}\big[P_1P_2\cdots P_N\big],\qquad P_j=|u_j\rangle\langle u_j|,\quad |u_{N+1}\rangle=|u_1\rangle$$

In a crystal, the loop is in k-space and the curvature fills the Brillouin zone.

For a band in a crystal, the cell-periodic Bloch state \(|u_{\mathbf k}\rangle\) plays the part of the spin state and the wavevector \(\mathbf k\) that of the field. In a two-band model, \(H(\mathbf k)=d_0+\mathbf d(\mathbf k)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), each \(\mathbf k\) gives a direction \(\mathbf n=\mathbf d/|\mathbf d|\), and the lower band is the spin state pointing against it. So the Berry curvature is the solid angle that \(\mathbf n\) sweeps per unit area of \(\mathbf k\), and its total over the zone counts how many times \(\mathbf n\) wraps the sphere: the Chern number, which quantizes the Hall conductance of the filled band.

$$\Omega(\mathbf k)=\partial_{k_x}A_y-\partial_{k_y}A_x=\tfrac12\,\mathbf n\cdot\big(\partial_{k_x}\mathbf n\times\partial_{k_y}\mathbf n\big),\qquad C=\frac1{2\pi}\iint_{\rm BZ}\Omega\,d^2k\in\mathbb Z,\qquad |\sigma_{xy}|=|C|\,\frac{e^2}{h}$$

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · A spin's loop

        The field sweeps once around the equator, slowly (100 Larmor periods). The spin follows it and ends where it began. What phase has it picked up, apart from the dynamical one?

        Show answer

        Minus π: the state comes back as \(-|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\), its sign flipped. The equator encloses half the sphere, \(\mathcal S=2\pi\), so \(\gamma=-\mathcal S/2=-\pi\); the lab measures \(-0.995\pi\), the last half per cent being the trip's finite speed. A spin's state needs two turns, not one, to come back to itself, and the sign is measurable: interfere a neutron whose spin has been carried around with one whose spin has not.

        Question 2 · A spin's loop

        A 60° cone, whose Berry phase is \(-\pi/2\). Go around it in a single Larmor period. What phase do you measure? How slowly must you go to get within 1% of \(-\pi/2\)?

        Show answer

        Only \(-0.245\pi\), half the Berry phase: the spin cannot keep up, it wobbles around the field and at one point has only a 75% chance of being in \(|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\). The error then falls like \(1/T\): \(-0.464\pi\) at 10 periods, \(-0.496\pi\) at 100, so about 75 periods for 1%. The probability of leaving the eigenstate falls faster, like \(1/T^2\): it is below 1% from 10 periods on, which is why "adiabatic" can look good long before the phase is right.

        Question 3 · A spin's loop

        Open the cone past the equator, to 120°. The loop now encloses three quarters of the sphere, \(\mathcal S=3\pi\). Is the Berry phase \(-3\pi/2\)? But the loop also encloses the southern cap, \(\mathcal S=\pi\), on its other side. Which is right?

        Show answer

        Both: \(-3\pi/2\) and \(+\pi/2\) (the southern cap is traversed the other way round) differ by exactly \(2\pi\), so they are the same phase, and the lab measures \(+0.504\pi\). This works only because the two choices of "inside" differ by \(4\pi\) of solid angle and the spin-½ phase is half of it. The Berry curvature of a spin is that of a magnetic monopole at the centre of the sphere, and this is Dirac's argument for why the charge of a monopole must be quantized.

        Question 4 · Chains

        Only three states, 120° apart, on the equator. How far is \(\gamma_3\) from the exact \(-\pi\)? And with three states on the 60° circle?

        Show answer

        On the equator, not at all: \(\gamma_3=-\pi\) exactly. The discrete product is exact, not for the smooth circle, but for the polygon joining the states by great-circle arcs: \(\gamma_N=-\tfrac12\times\) its solid angle. On the equator the arcs are the equator. On the 60° circle the triangle cuts the corners, \(\gamma_3=-0.318\pi\) against \(-\pi/2\), and the error falls like \(1/N^2\): \(-0.480\pi\) with 8 states, \(-0.499\pi\) with 30.

        Question 5 · Gauge

        Give every state a random phase, and press "New random phases" a few times. Which numbers change, and which do not?

        Show answer

        Every link phase changes, and so does the open-path sum, the phase collected from the first state to the last without closing the loop: these depend on the gauge and cannot be measured. The closed product, \(\gamma_N\), and the projector form \(-\arg\mathrm{Tr}[P_1\cdots P_N]\), which never refers to a phase at all, do not change in the least. The flags, which draw each state's phase as a direction (a phase \(\chi\) turns its flag by \(2\chi\)), point every which way.

        Question 6 · Gauge

        Now choose each state's phase so that its overlap with the previous one is real and positive: the video's parallel transport. Where has the Berry phase gone?

        Show answer

        Into the last link. Every overlap but the last now has zero phase, the flag is carried along each arc without turning, and when the chain closes, the last state does not match the first: the whole \(\gamma_N\) sits in that one link. You can move the Berry phase around between links, but never make it go away: back at the first point the flag has turned by the polygon's solid angle, \(-2\gamma_N\): the holonomy of the loop. It is the same arrow as the green one in tab 1, carried around without turning.

        Question 7 · Crystals

        Graphene with a sublattice mass \(M=0.2\,t\), as on boron nitride or a substrate that breaks the A–B symmetry. The loop (radius 0.8) around the valley K picks up \(-0.81\pi\). What does a loop around K′ pick up, and what is the Chern number?

        Show answer

        \(+0.81\pi\), and \(C=0\). Each valley holds a peak of Berry curvature worth almost \(\pm\pi\), a massive Dirac cone, but the two are opposite, as they must be in a crystal with time-reversal symmetry, \(\Omega(-\mathbf k)=-\Omega(\mathbf k)\). The total is zero, so there is no Hall effect; but valley K and valley K′ electrons are pushed sideways in opposite directions, the valley Hall effect. Take the mass to zero ("Graphene, no gap") and each valley's curvature collapses into a point, while a loop around it still picks up exactly π.

        Question 8 · Crystals

        Haldane's model: no sublattice mass, but a complex next-nearest-neighbour hopping, \(t_2=0.1\,t\), \(\varphi=90°\), which breaks time reversal with no net magnetic field. What is \(C\)? Now raise \(M\): at what value does \(C\) change, and how?

        Show answer

        \(C=1\): the two valleys' curvature now has the same sign, and \(\mathbf n\) wraps the sphere once. \(C\) stays 1 as \(M\) rises, until \(M=3\sqrt3\,t_2\sin\varphi=0.52\,t\): there the gap closes at K, and above it \(C=0\). An integer cannot change smoothly, so the gap must close for it to change; this is why a Chern insulator has to have gapless states at its edge, where it meets the vacuum, which has \(C=0\). The Qi–Wu–Zhang model does the same at \(m=0\) and \(\pm2\), where its gap closes.

        What is being computed?
        • The spin's loop: \(H(t)=-\tfrac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}\mathbf n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), with \(\mathbf n\) turning about \(z\) at a constant rate \(\omega=2\pi/T\) on a cone of half-angle \(\theta\). In the frame turning with the field the Hamiltonian is constant, \(H'=H(0)-\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z\), so the evolution is exact: \(|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-i\omega t\sigma_z/2}e^{-iH't/\hbar}|{+}\mathbf n(0)\rangle\), with no time steps. The measured phase is \(\arg\langle{+}\mathbf n(t)|\psi(t)\rangle-\omega_0t/2\), with \(|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\) in the gauge written above, followed continuously in time; it becomes the Berry phase when the trip is slow, and the probability \(|\langle{+}\mathbf n|\psi\rangle|^2\) shows how well the spin is following.
        • The green arrow: a tangent vector carried along the loop without turning about \(\mathbf n\) (parallel transport on the sphere). Relative to the local direction of decreasing latitude it turns by \(-\cos\theta\) per radian of \(\varphi\), and after a full loop it has turned by the enclosed solid angle \(\mathcal S\) (mod \(2\pi\)), twice the spin's phase and opposite in sign.
        • Chains of states: \(\gamma_N=-\arg\prod\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle\), computed in complex arithmetic, and \(-\arg\mathrm{Tr}[P_1\cdots P_N]\) from \(2\times2\) projectors. The smooth gauge is \(|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\) as written in tab 1; the random gauge multiplies each state by a random \(e^{i\chi_j}\); the parallel-transport gauge fixes each phase so that \(\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle\gt0\) for \(j\lt N\). Each flag is the real part of \(\psi^{T}(i\sigma_y)\boldsymbol\sigma\psi\), a unit vector at right angles to \(\mathbf n\) that turns by \(2\chi\) when the state's phase changes by \(\chi\). The orange slice runs from the north pole down the meridian \(\varphi=0\), along the equator to \(\varphi=\Delta\varphi\) and back up, enclosing \(\mathcal S=\Delta\varphi\).
        • The crystals: Qi–Wu–Zhang, \(\mathbf d=(\sin k_x,\ \sin k_y,\ m-\cos k_x-\cos k_y)\), lattice constant 1. The honeycomb: graphene's nearest-neighbour hopping \(t=1\) (carbon–carbon distance 1), \(d_x+id_y=\sum_j e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j}\) in the gauge with the A site at the origin, a sublattice mass \(M\sigma_z\), and Haldane's next-nearest-neighbour hopping \(t_2e^{\pm i\varphi}\), which gives \(d_0=2t_2\cos\varphi\sum_i\cos\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf b_i\) and adds \(-2t_2\sin\varphi\sum_i\sin\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf b_i\) to \(d_z\). The masses at the Dirac points are \(M\mp3\sqrt3\,t_2\sin\varphi\) at K and K′.
        • Curvature, connection and Chern number: \(\Omega=\mathbf d\cdot(\partial_{k_x}\mathbf d\times\partial_{k_y}\mathbf d)/2|\mathbf d|^3\) for the lower band, from analytic derivatives. The connection is drawn in two gauges: N, \(|u\rangle=(-e^{-i\phi}\sin\frac\vartheta2,\ \cos\frac\vartheta2)\) with \(\mathbf A_N=\sin^2\frac\vartheta2\,\nabla\phi\), smooth except where \(\mathbf n=-\hat z\); and S, \(\mathbf A_S=\mathbf A_N-\nabla\phi\), smooth except where \(\mathbf n=+\hat z\) (\(\vartheta,\phi\) are the angles of \(\mathbf n\)). The crossed circles mark the vortices, where the gauge fails. The Chern number uses Fukui, Hatsugai and Suzuki's method on a 48 × 48 grid over the unit cell of the reciprocal lattice: the sum of the Berry phases of the small plaquettes, which is an integer by construction. The loop's Berry phase uses 400 states on the circle; the flux of \(\Omega\) through it, Gauss–Legendre quadrature in the radius and 96 angles; \(\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf k\) in the chosen gauge, 2000 points.
        • Conventions: \(\mathbf A=i\langle u|\nabla u\rangle\), as in the video, so a state picks up \(e^{+i\gamma}\) with \(\gamma=\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf k\), and \(C=\frac1{2\pi}\int\Omega\). With these, the Qi–Wu–Zhang lower band has \(C=-1\) for \(0\lt m\lt2\) and \(+1\) for \(-2\lt m\lt0\); other sources may flip either sign. The sign of the Hall conductance depends further on the sign of the charge and the orientation of the axes, so only \(|\sigma_{xy}|\) is quoted.
        • Checks: the lab's loop phases agree with \(-\mathcal S/2\) and with the solid angles of the great-circle polygons to \(10^{-12}\); the adiabatic phase converges to \(-\mathcal S/2\) like \(1/T\); every loop's phase agrees with the flux of \(\Omega\) through it (Stokes) to \(10^{-4}\) or better, mod \(2\pi\); the Chern numbers agree with \(\frac1{2\pi}\int\Omega\) and with the masses at the Dirac points. The notebook repeats the calculation independently and asserts every number quoted in the answers.
