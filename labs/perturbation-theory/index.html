Perturbation Theory: Small Pushes, Order by Order

Most quantum problems cannot be solved exactly, but many are a solved problem plus a small push. Tilt a particle in a box and watch each order of perturbation theory close in on the exact energy, see the state borrow a little of its neighbours, then couple two levels and watch them repel and refuse to cross.

A solved problem, plus a small push

A particle in a box is one of the few problems with an exact answer: standing waves, with energies \(E_n^{(0)}=n^2E_1\). Now tilt the box with an electric field, or put a bump in it. There is no formula any more, but if the push is small, the answer cannot be far from the one we know. Perturbation theory finds it one order at a time: first the push averaged over the old state, then the extra the state gains by borrowing a little of its neighbours.

The lab measures lengths in the box width \(L\) and energies in the ground-state energy \(E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2\). For an electron in a 10 nm-wide GaAs quantum well, \(E_1\approx56\) meV, and a tilt of strength 1 is a field of about 56 kV/cm.

Perturbation
The small extra term V added to a Hamiltonian H⁰ we can already solve: here, the tilt, bump or step in the box.
Order
How many times the push enters a correction: first-order terms grow in proportion to the push, second-order ones as its square.
Matrix element
Vmn = ⟨m|V|n⟩: how strongly the push links state n to state m. Zero means the push cannot mix them at all.
Energy denominator
En − Em, the gap between two unperturbed levels. Nearby levels have small gaps, so they mix the most.
Intermediate normalisation
The choice ⟨n⁰|n⟩ = 1: keep the old state's share fixed and add the corrections at right angles to it. It simplifies the algebra; the state is normalised at the end.
Level repulsion
Two levels linked by a push move apart: the lower one goes down and the upper one up.
The math

Write \(H=H^{(0)}+\lambda V\) and expand both the energy and the state in powers of \(\lambda\), with intermediate normalisation \(\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle=1\), so that every correction is orthogonal to \(|n^{(0)}\rangle\). Collecting each power of \(\lambda\) in \(H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\) gives, with \(V_{mn}=\langle m^{(0)}|V|n^{(0)}\rangle\):

$$E_n^{(1)}=V_{nn},\qquad E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}},\qquad |n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{V_{mn}}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}\,|m^{(0)}\rangle$$
$$|n^{(2)}\rangle=\sum_{m\ne n}\Bigg[\sum_{k\ne n}\frac{V_{mk}V_{kn}}{(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})(E_n^{(0)}-E_k^{(0)})}-\frac{V_{nn}V_{mn}}{(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})^2}\Bigg]|m^{(0)}\rangle$$

The series makes sense only while every ratio \(|V_{mn}/(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})|\) is small: the push must be weak compared with the gaps to the levels it mixes. That is why this is non-degenerate perturbation theory.

Show

Each order brings the level closer.

Switch on a push of strength \(s\). To first order, a level moves by the push averaged over its old state. To second order, every other level nudges it, by \(|V_{mn}|^2/(E_n-E_m)\): a level below pushes it up, a level above pushes it down, and the closer the level, the harder it pushes.

What to notice: a tilt does nothing to the ground level at first order, because the state is symmetric and the tilt is not, yet the level still drops, at second order. The second-order curve hugs the exact one while the push is small, and drifts off when it is not.

The math

The unperturbed box has \(\psi_n=\sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L)\) and \(E_n^{(0)}=n^2E_1\). The tilt is \(V=s\,(x/L-\tfrac12)\), whose matrix elements are \(V_{mn}=-8mn\,s/\pi^2(m^2-n^2)^2\) when \(m+n\) is odd and zero otherwise, so \(V_{nn}=0\) and

$$E_n\approx n^2E_1+V_{nn}+\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}},\qquad E_1^{(2)}=-\frac{64\,s^2}{\pi^4}\sum_{m\ \mathrm{even}}\frac{m^2}{(m^2-1)^5}\approx-0.0108\,s^2$$

The state borrows a little of its neighbours.

To first order the new state is the old one plus a small amount of every other state, \(c_m=V_{mn}/(E_n-E_m)\): the same small numbers that set the second-order energy. Only states the push actually links to take part. A tilt, which is odd about the middle of the box, only mixes states of opposite symmetry.

What to notice: while the push is gentle, the corrected state lies almost exactly on the exact one. Intermediate normalisation keeps the old state's share fixed at 1, so the corrected state comes out slightly longer than 1; normalising it changes things only at second order.

The math

With \(\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle=1\), the corrections are orthogonal to \(|n^{(0)}\rangle\), and the length of the state is \(\langle n|n\rangle=1+\lambda^2\sum_{m\ne n}|c_m|^2+O(\lambda^3)\). Dividing by its square root leaves the share of the old state at

$$|\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle|^2_{\rm normalised}=\frac{1}{\langle n|n\rangle}\approx1-\lambda^2\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})^2}$$

Two coupled levels repel, and never cross.

The video's closing example: an electron in one of two quantum dots, \(|1\rangle\) on the left at \(E_1=-\delta/2\) and \(|2\rangle\) on the right at \(E_2=+\delta/2\), with tunnelling \(V\) between them. To second order the two levels move apart by \(V^2/|E_1-E_2|\) each: level repulsion. But tune the detuning \(\delta\) to zero and that divides by zero. The exact levels never cross: they keep a gap of \(2V\).

What to notice: far from the crossing, the electron stays in its own dot and perturbation theory is excellent. Near it, the electron is shared by both dots and the series fails; only the exact answer, or degenerate perturbation theory, gets it right.

The math
$$H=\begin{pmatrix}-\delta/2&V\\V&\delta/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\delta^2/4+V^2},\qquad E_1\approx-\frac\delta2+\frac{V^2}{E_1-E_2}=-\frac\delta2-\frac{V^2}{\delta}$$

Expanding the square root, \(\sqrt{\delta^2/4+V^2}=\tfrac{|\delta|}2+\tfrac{V^2}{|\delta|}-\tfrac{V^4}{|\delta|^3}+\dots\): perturbation theory is this expansion, and it converges only while \(2|V|<|\delta|\).

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · Energies

        Tilt the box with strength \(s=5\), with the particle in the ground state. To first order, does its energy go up or down? And to second order?

        Show answer

        To first order it does not move at all: the ground state is symmetric about the middle of the box and the tilt is antisymmetric, so the push averages to zero, \(V_{11}=0\). To second order it drops by \(0.0108\,s^2=0.271\), to \(0.729\,E_1\); the exact answer is \(0.741\,E_1\). The ground level always goes down at second order: every other level lies above it, so every term in the sum is negative.

        Question 2 · Energies

        Raise the right half of the box by \(s=2\). To first order, how much does each level rise? Which way does second order change it?

        Show answer

        Every level rises by exactly 1 at first order, half the step: each standing wave spends half its time in each half of the box. Second order then lowers the ground level by 0.25, to \(1.75\,E_1\) (exact \(1.766\,E_1\)), because the particle shifts towards the lower, left half; but it raises level 2, by 0.19, because level 1 below pushes it up harder than level 3 pushes it down.

        Question 3 · Energies

        Same tilt, \(s=5\), but now level 2. The ground level went down. Does level 2 go down too?

        Show answer

        No, it goes up, by 0.081 to second order (exact 0.069). Level 1 below pushes it up by 0.270, level 3 above pushes it down by 0.189, and the rest hardly matter. Level 1 wins: it is closer, \(4-1=3\) against \(9-4=5\). Plot B shows each push as a bar.

        Question 4 · The state

        With the tilt at \(s=5\), which other states does the ground state borrow from, and how much?

        Show answer

        Only the even ones, 2, 4, 6, …: the tilt is odd about the middle, so it links states of opposite symmetry and nothing else. Almost all of it comes from state 2, \(c_2=0.300\) to first order (exact 0.287), with \(c_4=0.005\) a distant second. Adding a little \(\psi_2\) to \(\psi_1\) shifts the particle towards the low side of the tilt.

        Question 5 · The state

        With intermediate normalisation, the first-order state is \(\psi_1+0.300\,\psi_2+\dots\), with the share of \(\psi_1\) fixed at 1. How long is it? Once normalised, how much of \(\psi_1\) is left?

        Show answer

        Its length is \(\sqrt{1+\sum|c_m|^2}=1.044\): a little more than 1, because the borrowed parts are at right angles to \(\psi_1\). Normalised, the share of \(\psi_1\) is \(1/1.090=0.917\), against 0.923 exactly. The change from 1 is of second order, \(\sum|c_m|^2\), which is why the first-order state needs no normalising at first order.

        Question 6 · The state

        Turn the tilt up to \(s=20\). How well does the first-order state match the exact one now?

        Show answer

        Badly: it borrows \(c_2=1.20\) of state 2, more than it keeps of state 1, where the exact state has 0.82. The small parameter is no longer small, and the overlap with the exact state falls to 0.903 (0.950 at second order). The energy fares worse still: second order gives \(-3.33\,E_1\) against the exact \(-1.97\,E_1\).

        Question 7 · Two levels

        The two dots' levels are \(\delta=1\) meV apart, with a tunnel coupling \(V=0.2\) meV. How far does each level move? Where is the electron in the lower state?

        Show answer

        They repel: the lower level moves down and the upper level up, by \(V^2/\delta=0.040\) meV each to second order; the exact shift is 0.0385 meV. The electron in the lower state is still 96.4% in the left dot, close to the 96.2% of first-order perturbation theory. The small parameter \(V/\delta=0.2\) really is small here.

        Question 8 · Two levelsChallenge

        Now tune the two levels to the same energy, \(\delta=0\). What does perturbation theory predict? What actually happens?

        Show answer

        Perturbation theory divides by \(E_1-E_2=0\) and predicts an infinite shift: non-degenerate perturbation theory breaks down. In reality the levels never cross. They keep a gap of \(2V=0.4\) meV, and both states are shared equally between the dots, \((|1\rangle\pm|2\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Sweeping \(\delta\) through zero, the lower state slides from one dot to the other: an avoided crossing. Equal levels need degenerate perturbation theory, which first diagonalises the push within the degenerate pair.

        What is being computed?
        • The box: \(0<x<L\), with \(\psi_n=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)\) and \(E_n^{(0)}=n^2E_1\). Strengths \(s\) are in units of \(E_1\). The tilt is \(s\,(x/L-\tfrac12)\); the bump is \(s\) over the middle tenth of the box; the step is \(s\) over the right half.
        • Matrix elements: exact formulas. For the tilt, \(V_{mn}=-8mn\,s/\pi^2(m^2-n^2)^2\) when \(m+n\) is odd; for the bump and step, the integral of \(2\sin(m\pi x)\sin(n\pi x)\) over the raised region.
        • Perturbation theory: the first- and second-order energies and states from the formulas in the math box, summing over the first 40 box states, with intermediate normalisation \(\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle=1\). Plot B of the first tab shows each term of the second-order sum.
        • The exact answer: the Hamiltonian \(E_n^{(0)}\delta_{mn}+V_{mn}\) in the same 40 states, diagonalised numerically (Householder reduction and the QL algorithm). In one dimension levels never cross, so the exact level \(n\) is simply the \(n\)-th lowest. The notebook checks these energies against a finite-difference solution with 4000 grid points; they agree to better than \(10^{-3}\,E_1\).
        • Overlaps: \(|\langle\psi_{\rm exact}|\psi_{\rm approx}\rangle|^2\) after normalising both. The share of the old state is \(|\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle|^2\) for the normalised exact state, against \(1/(1+\sum|c_m^{(1)}|^2)\) from perturbation theory.
        • Two levels: \(H=\begin{pmatrix}-\delta/2&V\\V&\delta/2\end{pmatrix}\), with exact levels \(\pm\sqrt{\delta^2/4+V^2}\) and the lower state's weight on the left dot \(\tfrac12(1+\delta/\sqrt{\delta^2+4V^2})\). Perturbation theory gives \(\mp(\delta/2+V^2/\delta)\) and a first-order state that keeps \(1/(1+V^2/\delta^2)\) of the unperturbed one.
        • Checks: the matrix elements agree with numerical integration to \(10^{-10}\); at weak pushes perturbation theory and the exact answer differ only at third order and above; and the numbers quoted in the questions are asserted in the notebook.
