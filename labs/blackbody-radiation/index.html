Counting light modes, then filling them

Light inside a hot cavity is a set of standing-wave modes, and the number of modes per unit wavelength grows as \(1/\lambda^4\). Classical physics gives every mode the same average energy \(k_BT\), which yields the Rayleigh–Jeans law. Planck instead let each mode hold energy only in quanta \(hc/\lambda\), so short-wavelength modes are rarely excited:

$$B_\lambda^{\rm RJ}(T)=\frac{2ck_BT}{\lambda^4},\qquad B_\lambda(T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1},\qquad \frac{\langle E\rangle}{k_BT}=\frac{x}{e^{x}-1},\quad x=\frac{hc}{\lambda k_BT}$$

\(B_\lambda\) is the spectral radiance, in W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹ per unit wavelength. The energy density inside the cavity is \(u_\lambda=4\pi B_\lambda/c\). The occupation \(1/(e^x-1)\) is the Bose–Einstein distribution for photons.

    Step 2 · Predict, then test

    Predict first

    Load each example, make a prediction, then check it with the readouts and plots.

    Question 1

    At 4000 K, where is the peak, and how much of the radiance falls in the visible range?

    Show answer

    The peak is at 724 nm, right at the red edge of the visible. Only about 26% of the radiance is visible; most of the rest is infrared. The glow looks warm orange-white.

    Question 2

    The Sun's surface is about 5772 K. Compared with 4000 K, where does the peak move, and how much more power does each square metre radiate?

    Show answer

    The peak moves to 502 nm, in the blue-green, close to where our eyes are most sensitive. The power per area rises by \((5772/4000)^4 \approx 4.3\) times, to about 63 MW/m². About 44% of the radiance is now visible, so sunlight looks nearly white.

    Question 3

    Rayleigh–Jeans agrees with Planck at long wavelengths. At 4000 K, how far off is it at 3000 nm, and at 400 nm?

    Show answer

    At 3000 nm it is 1.9 times too high; at 400 nm, 894 times too high. Integrated over all wavelengths it predicts infinite power: the ultraviolet catastrophe. At the peak it is always 28.7 times too high, whatever the temperature, because the peak always sits at the same value of \(x=hc/\lambda k_BT\approx 4.97\).

    Question 4

    You are at about 310 K. Do you glow?

    Show answer

    Yes, in the infrared. Your spectrum peaks near 9.3 µm and your skin radiates about 520 W/m², though you absorb most of that back from your surroundings. The visible share is about 5 × 10⁻²³, far too little to see. Thermal cameras image exactly this light.

    Question 5

    The 3000 K curve is pinned. Raise the temperature to 6000 K. What happens to the peak wavelength, the peak height and the total power?

    Show answer

    The peak wavelength halves, from 966 to 483 nm. The peak height rises 32 times (\(T^5\)) and the total power 16 times (\(T^4\)). The area under the curve grows more slowly than its height because the curve also gets narrower.

    Question 6

    In the lower plot, at what wavelength does each mode carry only half the classical kBT?

    Show answer

    At about 2.86 µm, where \(x/(e^x-1)=\tfrac12\), that is \(x\approx1.26\). At shorter wavelengths a single quantum \(hc/\lambda\) costs more than the thermal energy \(k_BT\) available, so those modes are rarely excited. This freezing-out is what removes the ultraviolet catastrophe.

    Download notebook .ipynb · 9 KB
    What is being computed?
    • Spectra: Planck's law and the Rayleigh–Jeans law exactly as written above, with the exact SI constants \(h\), \(c\) and \(k_B\). The Bose–Einstein factor uses expm1, which computes \(e^x-1\) accurately even when \(x\) is small.
    • Readouts: Wien's law \(\lambda_{\max}=b/T\) with \(b=2.8978\) mm·K; the power per area \(\sigma T^4\) with \(\sigma=5.6704\times10^{-8}\) W·m⁻²·K⁻⁴; and the visible share, from integrating \(B_\lambda\) over 380–750 nm (Simpson's rule) and dividing by \(\sigma T^4/\pi\).
    • Colour: the spectrum is weighted by the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (the analytic fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley, 2013), converted to sRGB, and scaled to full brightness. It shows the hue only; below about 800 K the real glow is too faint to see.
    • Model limits: an ideal blackbody, which absorbs everything that falls on it. Real surfaces emit less by a factor called the emissivity, which depends on wavelength.
