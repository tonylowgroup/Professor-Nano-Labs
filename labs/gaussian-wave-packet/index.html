A particle built from plane waves

A single plane wave \(e^{ikx}\) has one momentum but is spread over all space. Adding many of them, each weighted by a Gaussian \(\Phi(k)\), produces a packet localized near \(x = 0\). Each plane wave then evolves with its own frequency \(\omega_k = \hbar k^2/2m\), and this lab adds them up again at every moment:

$$\Psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,e^{-i\omega_k t}\,dk,\qquad \Phi(k)=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{\sigma}\;e^{-\sigma^2(k-k_0)^2}$$

Here \(\sigma\) is the initial standard deviation of \(|\Psi(x,0)|^2\) and \(k_0\) is the central wavevector.

Predict first

Load each example, make a prediction before pressing Play, then check it against the lab.

Question 1

The packet starts with σ = 0.71 nm and k₀ = 0, for a particle of mass 0.5 m₀. Will it move? Roughly how wide will it be after 50 fs?

Show answer

It does not move: with k₀ = 0 the average momentum is zero. It does spread, as \(\sigma(t)=\sigma\sqrt{1+(t/\tau)^2}\) with spreading time \(\tau = 2m\sigma^2/\hbar \approx 4.3\) fs. After 50 fs the width is about 8.2 nm, nearly 12 times wider, and the peak density is 12 times lower. The total probability stays 1; about 98.5% of it is still inside the ±20 nm view.

Question 2

Halve the initial width to σ = 0.35 nm. After 50 fs, will this packet be narrower or wider than the one in Question 1?

Show answer

Wider, about 16.4 nm compared with 8.2 nm. A narrower packet is built from a wider range of wavevectors (\(\Delta p = \hbar/2\sigma\)), so its parts move apart faster. The spreading time drops four times, to about 1.1 fs, and at late times the width grows as \(\hbar t/2m\sigma\). Squeezing a particle harder makes it spread faster.

Question 3

Keep σ = 0.71 nm but raise the mass from 0.5 m₀ to 2 m₀. How does the spreading change?

Show answer

It slows down. The spreading time grows in proportion to the mass, from 4.3 fs to about 17.3 fs, and after 50 fs the width is about 2.2 nm instead of 8.2 nm. This is why everyday objects never visibly spread: for a 145 g baseball located to within a micrometre, τ is about 6,000 times the age of the universe.

Question 4

Now k₀ = 3 nm⁻¹ and m = m₀, so the packet moves to the right. Follow one crest of Re Ψ. Does it travel with the packet?

Show answer

No. The envelope moves at the group velocity \(v_g = \hbar k_0/m \approx 0.35\) nm/fs, about 17 nm in 50 fs. Each crest moves at the phase velocity \(v_p = \omega/k = \hbar k_0/2m\), only half as fast. So crests appear at the front of the packet, drift backward through it, and fade out at the rear. Use the time slider to step through slowly.

Question 5

Replace ω(k) by its straight-line approximation around k₀, \(\omega \approx \omega_0 + v_g (k-k_0)\). What happens to the spreading?

Show answer

It disappears. Every plane wave now has the same group velocity, so the whole packet slides along rigidly at \(v_g\), crests included. Spreading comes entirely from the curvature of the dispersion, \(d^2\omega/dk^2 = \hbar/m\), which is also why the spreading time depends on the mass.

Question 6

Use only 15 evenly spaced plane waves instead of a continuous spread. What appears in the probability density?

Show answer

Copies of the packet. A sum of plane waves spaced by Δk repeats itself every \(2\pi/\Delta k\) in position. Here Δk ≈ 0.21 nm⁻¹, so copies sit about 29 nm apart. Adding more plane waves makes Δk smaller and pushes the copies out of view; only a continuous range of k gives a single, localized particle. A single plane wave (N = 1) is spread evenly over all space.

What is being computed?

The lab evaluates the plane-wave sum directly, with no closed-form shortcut. On a grid of wavevectors \(k_j\) spaced by \(\Delta k\), with trapezoidal weights \(w_j\) (½ at the two ends, 1 elsewhere),

$$\Psi(x_i,t)\approx\frac{\Delta k}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_j w_j\,\Phi(k_j)\,e^{ik_jx_i}\,e^{-i\omega_{k_j}t}.$$
  • Full dispersion: \(\omega_k=\hbar k^2/2m\). Linearized: \(\omega_k\approx\omega_{k_0}+v_g(k-k_0)\), with \(v_g=\hbar k_0/m\).
  • Converged sum: wavevectors span \(k_0\pm 8/\sigma\), where \(\Phi\) has fallen to \(e^{-64}\) of its peak. The spacing \(\Delta k\) is chosen so that the repeated copies at \(2\pi/\Delta k\) stay outside the view for the whole time range.
  • Few plane waves: N evenly spaced wavevectors span \(k_0\pm 3/\sigma\). With N = 1 the single plane wave is drawn with the packet's initial peak amplitude.
  • Tiles: the centre, width, spreading time and \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\) are the exact free-particle results \(\langle x\rangle = v_g t\), \(\sigma(t)=\sigma\sqrt{1+(t/\tau)^2}\), \(\tau = 2m\sigma^2/\hbar\) and \(\Delta x\,\Delta p = \tfrac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{1+(t/\tau)^2}\). "Inside the view" integrates the computed \(|\Psi|^2\) over the window and is never renormalized.
  • Constants: \(\hbar/m_0 = 0.115768\ \text{nm}^2/\text{fs}\) (CODATA 2018). Units: x in nm, t in fs, k in nm⁻¹, Ψ in nm⁻¹ᐟ².
  • Model limits: one dimension, no potential (a free particle), non-relativistic, spin ignored.

The defaults (σ = 1/√2 nm, m = 0.5 m₀, ±20 nm, 0–50 fs) match the companion Jupyter notebook.
