Graphene Band Structure

Graphene is a single sheet of carbon atoms in a honeycomb. Let each electron hop between neighbouring atoms and its band structure follows: two bands that touch at just six points, the corners of the Brillouin zone. Near those points the electrons behave like massless relativistic particles. Build the bands from tight binding, explore them in 3D, then zoom in on the Dirac cones.

Two atoms per cell, one hop at a time

The honeycomb is a triangular lattice with two carbon atoms in each cell, A and B. Every A atom has three B neighbours, at \(\boldsymbol\delta_1,\boldsymbol\delta_2,\boldsymbol\delta_3\), a distance \(a=0.142\) nm away. In the tight-binding model an electron in a carbon \(p_z\) orbital hops to a neighbour with amplitude \(-t\), \(t\approx2.7\) eV. A Bloch wave \((c_A,c_B)\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\) then obeys a 2×2 eigenvalue problem:

$$\begin{pmatrix}0&-t\,g(\mathbf k)\\-t\,g^*(\mathbf k)&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_A\\c_B\end{pmatrix}=E\begin{pmatrix}c_A\\c_B\end{pmatrix},\qquad g(\mathbf k)=\sum_{j=1}^{3}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j},\qquad E_\pm(\mathbf k)=\pm t\,|g(\mathbf k)|$$
$$|g(\mathbf k)|^2=3+2\cos\!\left(\sqrt3k_ya\right)+4\cos\!\left(\tfrac32k_xa\right)\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}k_ya\right)$$

The bands touch where \(g(\mathbf k)=0\): at the two inequivalent corners of the Brillouin zone, \(\mathbf K\) and \(\mathbf K'\). Expanding \(g\) to first order in \(\mathbf q=\mathbf k-\mathbf K\) gives the Dirac Hamiltonian, with a velocity set by the hopping and the bond length:

$$H_{\mathbf K+\mathbf q}\approx\hbar v_F\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-i\pi/6}(q_x\pm iq_y)\\e^{i\pi/6}(q_x\mp iq_y)&0\end{pmatrix}\ \text{(upper signs at }\mathbf K\text{, lower at }\mathbf K'\text{; the phase depends on the axes)},\qquad E=\pm\hbar v_F|\mathbf q|,\qquad v_F=\frac{3ta}{2\hbar}\approx0.87\times10^6\ \text{m/s}$$

Three hops, three phases.

An electron on an A atom can hop to three B neighbours. In a Bloch wave each hop picks up a phase \(e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j}\), and the energy is \(t\) times the length of their sum. At \(\Gamma\) the three phases agree and add up to 3. At the zone corner K they point 120° apart and cancel, so the two bands meet at zero energy. Move \(\mathbf k\) along the path and watch the arrows, and the colours of the atoms, which show the phase of the wavefunction on each site.

Two bands, six cones.

Across the whole Brillouin zone the lower band (π, filled in neutral graphene) and the upper band (π*, empty) meet only at the six corners. Two corners are inequivalent: K and K′, the two valleys. Raise the Fermi level and the Fermi surface grows from points into small circles around each corner, then distorts, and at \(E_F=t\) the pieces join at the M points, where the density of states diverges.

$$\rho(E)=\frac{g_sg_v\,|E|}{2\pi\hbar^2v_F^2}\ \ (\text{near }E=0),\qquad n=\frac{E_F^2}{\pi\hbar^2v_F^2},\qquad g_s=g_v=2$$

Massless electrons with a pseudospin.

Near each corner the bands form a cone, \(E=\pm\hbar v_F|\mathbf q|\), with the same slope in every direction: electrons move at \(v_F\) whatever their energy, like photons. The two components \((c_A,c_B)\) behave like a spin, the pseudospin, which is locked to the direction of motion: as \(\mathbf q\) goes once round the corner, the pseudospin turns once too, in opposite senses at K and K′. That winding gives a Berry phase of \(\pi\). Further from the corner the cone bends and turns triangular (trigonal warping). A difference \(2\Delta\) between the A and B sites opens a gap and gives the electrons a mass.

$$E=\pm\sqrt{\Delta^2+\hbar^2v_F^2q^2},\qquad m^*=\frac{\Delta}{v_F^2},\qquad \gamma_\text{Berry}=\pm\pi\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{E}\right)$$

      Step 2 · Predict, then test

      Predict first

      Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

      Question 1 · Tight binding

      At the centre of the zone, \(\mathbf k=0\), what are the two energies, and how do the A and B atoms compare in each state?

      Show answer

      \(g(0)=3\), so \(E=\pm3t=\pm8.1\) eV, the bottom and top of the π bands. In the lower (bonding) state every atom has the same phase; in the upper (antibonding) state the A and B atoms are opposite. The band is \(6t\approx16\) eV wide.

      Question 2 · Tight binding

      At the zone corner K, why is the energy exactly zero, whatever the value of \(t\)?

      Show answer

      The three phases \(e^{i\mathbf K\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j}\) are \(1\), \(e^{2\pi i/3}\) and \(e^{-2\pi i/3}\): three arrows 120° apart that close into a triangle, so \(g(\mathbf K)=0\). The waves hopping in from the three neighbours interfere destructively. That is symmetry, not a coincidence of numbers, so it survives any change of \(t\) (and \(t'\)), but not a difference between the A and B sites.

      Question 3 · Tight binding

      At M, halfway between two corners, what are the energies?

      Show answer

      \(|g(\mathbf M)|=1\), so \(E=\pm t=\pm2.7\) eV. M is a saddle point: the band rises towards Γ and falls towards K. Saddle points pile up states, so the density of states diverges there (a van Hove singularity); see the next tab.

      Question 4 · The bands

      Dope graphene so the Fermi level sits 0.3 eV above the Dirac point. What does the Fermi surface look like, and how many electrons does that take?

      Show answer

      Six small circles at the corners, a third of each inside the zone: two whole circles, one per valley, of radius \(k_F=E_F/\hbar v_F=0.52\) nm⁻¹. The density is \(n=E_F^2/\pi\hbar^2v_F^2\approx8.7\times10^{12}\) cm⁻², about one extra electron for every 440 carbon atoms.

      Question 5 · The bands

      Keep raising \(E_F\) until it reaches \(t=2.7\) eV. What happens to the Fermi surface?

      Show answer

      The circles grow into triangles, and at \(E_F=t\) they touch at the six M points, forming a hexagon. Above that, the Fermi surface closes around Γ instead of around the corners: a change of topology called a Lifshitz transition. The density of states diverges at that energy, and the electron density there is about \(9.5\times10^{14}\) cm⁻², a quarter of an extra electron per atom.

      Question 6 · The bands

      In neutral graphene the Fermi level is at the Dirac point. How many states are there at that energy? Is graphene a metal or an insulator?

      Show answer

      None: the density of states vanishes linearly at \(E=0\), so there is no gap but no Fermi surface either. Graphene is a semimetal (a zero-gap semiconductor). This is why a gate can tune its carrier density, and its conductance, so easily.

      Question 7 · The Dirac cone

      How good is the Dirac model? Compare the true contour with the circle \(|\mathbf q|=E/\hbar v_F\) at 0.1 eV and at 1 eV.

      Show answer

      At 0.1 eV the contour is very nearly a circle: its largest and smallest radii differ by 1.2% (the lab's "trigonal warping"), and its average radius matches \(E/\hbar v_F\) to 0.01%. At 1 eV it has turned into a rounded triangle: the radii differ by about 14%, and the average is 1% above the Dirac value. The Dirac model is excellent for the energies reached in devices, which are well below 1 eV.

      Question 8 · The Dirac cone

      Put +0.3 eV on the A atoms and −0.3 eV on the B atoms. What happens to the cone, the velocity and the Berry phase?

      Show answer

      A gap of \(2\Delta=0.6\) eV opens, and the cone rounds off at the bottom: \(E=\sqrt{\Delta^2+\hbar^2v_F^2q^2}\), a particle with mass \(m^*=\Delta/v_F^2\approx0.069\,m_e\). Near the band edge the pseudospin tilts out of the plane (onto one sublattice), and the Berry phase drops from \(\pi\) to \(\pi(1-\Delta/E)\): \(\pi/2\) at \(E=0.6\) eV.

      What is being computed?
      • Lattice: carbon–carbon distance \(a=0.142\) nm, neighbour vectors \(\boldsymbol\delta_{1,2}=a(\tfrac12,\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})\), \(\boldsymbol\delta_3=a(-1,0)\), lattice vectors \(\mathbf a_{1,2}=a(\tfrac32,\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})\), and \(\mathbf K,\mathbf K'=\tfrac{2\pi}{3a}(1,\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3})\). These are the values used in the graphene transport lab.
      • Bands: the 2×2 Bloch Hamiltonian, with the optional second-neighbour hopping \(t'\) and sublattice energy \(\pm\Delta\): \(E_\pm=-t'|g|^2\pm\sqrt{\Delta^2+t^2|g|^2}\), measured from the Dirac point. The six second neighbours contribute \(-t'(|g|^2-3)\); the constant \(3t'\) is dropped.
      • Wavefunctions: the eigenvector \((c_A,c_B)\), with the amplitude on an atom at \(\mathbf r\) equal to \(c_s e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\). Its overall phase is arbitrary; only the differences between atoms matter.
      • Density of states: a histogram of both bands over about 190,000 points of the Brillouin zone, lightly smoothed. It agrees with the exact formula (an elliptic integral) to about 1% over most of the band, and within a few percent below 0.5 eV, where the density of states is small; the value at EF quoted near the Dirac point is computed exactly instead. The carrier density comes from the exact area inside the Fermi contours while they surround K and K′, and from the density of states beyond that.
      • Dirac model: \(E=\pm\sqrt{\Delta^2+\hbar^2v_F^2q^2}\) with \(v_F=3ta/2\hbar\). Contours of the tight-binding bands are found by following rays out from each corner to the first crossing (360 directions for the numbers shown, fewer for the drawings).
      • Pseudospin and Berry phase: the pseudospin is the direction of \(\mathbf d\) in \(H=\mathbf d\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\); the Berry phase is \(-\arg\prod_i\langle u_i|u_{i+1}\rangle\) around the contour. Which valley turns which way depends on how A, B and the axes are labelled; that the two valleys turn opposite ways does not. With no gap the Berry phase is π in both valleys (+π and −π are the same phase); with Δ > 0 it is \(\pm\pi(1-\Delta/E)\), with opposite signs at K and K′.
