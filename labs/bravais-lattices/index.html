Crystal = lattice + basis

A Bravais lattice is the set of translations that leave a crystal unchanged. Three primitive vectors generate every lattice point, and a basis of \(N_b\) atoms at positions \(\mathbf p_\alpha\) is repeated at each one:

$$\mathbf R=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3,\quad n_i\in\mathbb Z,\qquad \mathbf r_{n_1n_2n_3,\alpha}=\mathbf R+\mathbf p_\alpha,\qquad N_{\text{atoms, conventional}}=N_{\text{lattice points, conventional}}\times N_b$$

Two layers of examples. Every lattice has ideal one- and two-atom teaching models. The real materials, chosen for their place in semiconductor and device technology, give two cases per lattice (one for base-centred orthorhombic), plus GaAs and GaN, each with its actual primitive-basis size rather than a forced one/two pairing. Basis size always means the number of atoms in a primitive cell of the complete crystal, not the number of chemical elements, and not the number of spheres drawn in a conventional cell.

Step 2 · Predict, then test

Predict first

Each button loads a structure into the viewer. Predict the answer, then check the counts in the material card.

Question 1

Copper, silicon and gallium arsenide all have a face-centred cubic (cF) lattice. How many atoms does each have in its primitive basis, and in the conventional cube?

Show answer

Copper: 1 basis atom, 4 atoms per cube. Silicon: 2 identical atoms, 8 per cube. GaAs: 2 different atoms, one Ga and one As, also 8 per cube. Same lattice, different bases. Silicon's "diamond" structure is not a Bravais lattice: it is cF plus a two-atom basis, and zincblende GaAs is the same with the two basis sites filled by different elements.

Question 2

Tungsten and caesium iodide both look like a cube with an atom in the middle. Are they both body-centred cubic?

Show answer

No. Tungsten is body-centred cubic (cI) with a one-atom basis. CsI is primitive cubic (cP) with a two-atom basis: the centre is iodine and the corners are caesium. A lattice translation must carry every atom onto the same kind of atom, and the shift (½, ½, ½) moves Cs onto I, so it is not a lattice vector.

Question 3

Ruthenium (hexagonal close-packed) and wurtzite GaN both sit on a primitive hexagonal (hP) lattice. How many basis atoms does each have?

Show answer

Ruthenium has 2, so hexagonal close-packed is hP plus a two-atom basis, not a Bravais lattice of its own. Wurtzite GaN has 4: two Ga and two N. Two chemical elements do not mean two basis atoms.

Question 4

Black phosphorus and graphite are each made of a single element. Does one element mean a one-atom basis?

Show answer

No. Both have four atoms in the primitive basis. Black phosphorus has puckered layers on a base-centred orthorhombic (oC) lattice. Graphite has flat honeycomb layers on a primitive hexagonal (hP) lattice, two per cell, each shifted against the other (AB stacking). "One element" and "one-atom basis" are different statements: atoms of the same element can sit at positions that no lattice translation connects.

Question 5

Strontium titanate and barium titanate are both perovskites with five atoms per cell. Do they share a Bravais lattice?

Show answer

No. SrTiO₃ is cubic (cP). At room temperature BaTiO₃ is stretched about 1% along c (c/a ≈ 1.01) and its Ti sits slightly off-centre, so its lattice is tetragonal (tP). That small distortion is what makes BaTiO₃ ferroelectric and useful in capacitors. Look closely at the Ti position in both.

Question 6

With boundary copies on, silicon's conventional cube shows 18 spheres. Why does the card say 8 atoms per conventional cell?

Show answer

Atoms on the boundary are shared with neighbouring cells. The 8 corner atoms each count 1/8 (1 in total), the 6 face atoms each count ½ (3 in total), and the 4 atoms inside count fully: 1 + 3 + 4 = 8. Untick Boundary copies and the viewer draws exactly those 8.

What is being shown?
  • Structures: every material's atomic positions were expanded from its space group when the notebook was prepared and are stored in full, so nothing is fitted in the browser. Coordinates are rounded, full-occupancy models; thermal motion, defects, dopants and surfaces are not included.
  • Checks: the notebook verifies atom counts, centring translations, cell volumes, and that no unexpected extra translation makes a stated basis non-primitive. These test internal consistency; they are not an independent check of an experimental phase assignment.
  • Drawing: the 3D view is drawn directly on a canvas, with no external libraries. Rotation moves the camera, not the atoms. Sphere sizes are illustrative, not ionic radii, and the nearest-distance guides are geometric aids, not chemical bonds.
  • Cells: for hR the conventional cell uses hexagonal axes and the primitive cell is rhombohedral. For hP the viewer uses the primitive 120° parallelepiped, not the larger hexagonal prism.
  • Roles: each material card separates semiconductors, insulating device components, metallic contacts and interconnects, precursors, and structural references. A precursor's structure is not the structure of the device made from it.

Main structural references: the IUCr Bravais-lattice nomenclature and the AFLOW crystal-prototype encyclopedia (Mehl et al., Comput. Mater. Sci. 136, S1 (2017); Hicks et al., 199, 110450 (2021)). Each material card links its own sources.
