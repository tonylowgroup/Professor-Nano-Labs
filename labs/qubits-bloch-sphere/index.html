A qubit is a point on a sphere

A qubit has two basis states, \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\), and its state is a superposition of them with two complex amplitudes. Normalization and an overall phase, which no measurement can detect, leave two real numbers: the angles \(\theta\) and \(\varphi\). So every pure state is a point on a unit sphere:

$$|\psi\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\vec r=\begin{pmatrix}\sin\theta\cos\varphi\\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi\\ \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}=\langle\psi|\vec\sigma|\psi\rangle$$

Opposite points are orthogonal states: \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) at the poles, \(|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) on the x axis and \(|\pm i\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) on the y axis. The angle \(\theta\) sets the measurement probabilities; \(\varphi\) is the relative phase between the two amplitudes.

Every single-qubit gate turns the sphere.

Pick a starting state, then add gates. Each one rotates the arrow about its own axis, and the replay shows the path. The phasor plot shows the two amplitudes themselves, including the overall phase that the sphere hides.

$$R_{\hat n}(\alpha)=e^{-i\alpha\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma/2}=\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}\,I-i\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma,\qquad X=iR_x(\pi),\quad H=iR_{(\hat x+\hat z)/\sqrt2}(\pi),\quad S=e^{i\pi/4}R_z\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$$

You never see the arrow. You only get shots.

A measurement along an axis \(\hat n\) gives +1 or −1, nothing in between, and each shot uses a fresh copy of the state. The probabilities come from the arrow; counting shots in three directions rebuilds it.

$$P(\pm\hat n)=\frac{1\pm\vec r\cdot\hat n}{2},\qquad \hat r_n=\frac{N_+-N_-}{N},\qquad \delta\hat r_n\approx\sqrt{\frac{1-\hat r_n^{\,2}}{N}}$$

Entangle two qubits. Their arrows shrink.

Each sphere shows one qubit on its own. For a product state both arrows reach the surface. Entanglement pulls them inside: a qubit that is part of a Bell pair has no direction of its own at all.

$$|\psi\rangle=\sum_{a,b}c_{ab}|ab\rangle,\qquad \rho_A=\mathrm{Tr}_B|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=\frac{I+\vec r_A\cdot\vec\sigma}{2},\qquad C=2\,|c_{00}c_{11}-c_{01}c_{10}|,\qquad |\vec r_A|=|\vec r_B|=\sqrt{1-C^2}$$

      Step 2 · Predict, then test

      Predict first

      Load each example, make a prediction, then add the gate, replay the circuit or take shots to check it.

      Question 1 · Gates

      One H has taken |0⟩ to |+⟩. Where does a second H take the arrow, and which way does it go around?

      Show answer

      Back to |0⟩. H is a half turn about the diagonal axis halfway between x and z, so two of them make one full turn: \(H^2=I\). The trail is a single tilted circle around that axis, through |0⟩ and |+⟩; the first H passes the −y side and the second the +y side.

      Question 2 · Gates

      H and \(R_y(90°)\) both take |0⟩ to |+⟩. Are they the same gate? Predict what each one does to |+⟩, then add them one at a time (Undo in between).

      Show answer

      No. H sends |+⟩ back up to |0⟩, but \(R_y(90°)\) carries it on down to |1⟩. Two gates can agree on one input and differ on others. On the sphere the difference is plain: H is a half turn about the x–z diagonal, \(R_y(90°)\) a quarter turn about y.

      Question 3 · Gates

      Rotate |0⟩ a full 360° about x. The arrow comes back to where it started. Is the state the same?

      Show answer

      The arrow is, the amplitudes are not: watch the phasor plot. \(R_x(2\pi)=-I\), so α ends at −1 instead of +1. An overall sign never changes a probability, so for one qubit the two are the same physical state. It takes 720° for the amplitudes to return too. That sign matters once the qubit is part of something bigger, for example as the target of a controlled rotation.

      Question 4 · Measure

      The arrow is tilted 60° from |0⟩ toward |+⟩. What fraction of shots along z will give |0⟩? Along x, |+⟩? If you take 100 shots along z, how far from your prediction might the count land?

      Show answer

      \(\vec r=(\sin 60°,0,\cos 60°)=(0.866,0,0.5)\). Along z, \(P(0)=(1+0.5)/2=0.75\); along x, \(P(+)=(1+0.866)/2=0.933\); along y, 0.5. With 100 shots along z expect 75, give or take \(\sqrt{100\times0.75\times0.25}\approx4.3\), so counts from about 66 to 84 are normal.

      Question 5 · Measure

      |+⟩ and |+i⟩ both give 50/50 along z. If someone hands you one of them, how can you tell which?

      Show answer

      Measure along y: |+i⟩ gives +i every time, while |+⟩ gives 50/50. Along x it is the other way round. Shots along one axis reveal only the arrow's component on that axis, so rebuilding an unknown state takes all three: state tomography.

      Question 6 · Two qubits

      Qubit A is in |+⟩ and B in |0⟩. Add a CNOT from A to B. What happens to each arrow? Then measure both along z, and both along x. Could two classical coins that always land the same way do the same?

      Show answer

      Both arrows shrink to the centre: this is the Bell state \((|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt2\), with C = 1 and one bit of entanglement entropy. Each qubit alone is a fair coin, yet along z the two outcomes always agree. Classical coins could do that much. But along x they also always agree, and along y they always disagree (\(\langle YY\rangle=-1\)). A classical mix of 00 and 11 would give random, uncorrelated results along x.

      Question 7 · Two qubits

      Both qubits are in |+⟩. Will a CNOT from A to B entangle them?

      Show answer

      No: nothing changes at all. The CNOT applies X to B when A is 1, and \(X|+\rangle=|+\rangle\). Both arrows stay on the surface and C stays 0. The same gate fully entangles |+⟩|0⟩, so whether a CNOT entangles depends on its input.

      Question 8 · Two qubits

      A SWAP exchanges |0⟩|1⟩ into |1⟩|0⟩, and neither the start nor the end is entangled. Halfway through the gate, are the qubits entangled?

      Show answer

      Yes, completely. Halfway, \(\sqrt{\mathrm{SWAP}}\,|01\rangle=\tfrac{1+i}{2}|01\rangle+\tfrac{1-i}{2}|10\rangle\), which has C = 1. Both arrows shrink to zero mid-gate and grow back with A and B exchanged. That is why \(\sqrt{\mathrm{SWAP}}\), produced by switching on the exchange interaction between two electron spins, can serve as the entangling gate for spin qubits.

      What is being computed?
      • States: exact complex vectors, two amplitudes for one qubit and four for two. Nothing is approximated, and every state is pure: there is no noise or decoherence in this lab, so arrows shrink only through entanglement.
      • Gates: each named gate is written as a phase times a rotation, \(U=e^{i\gamma}R_{\hat n}(\alpha)\); for example \(X=iR_x(\pi)\), \(H=iR_{(\hat x+\hat z)/\sqrt2}(\pi)\) and \(T=e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\pi/4)\). The replay follows \(U(s)=e^{i\gamma s}R_{\hat n}(\alpha s)\) for \(0\le s\le1\), which is the principal fractional power \(U^s\): the state partway through a gate pulse. A rotation gate \(R_{\hat n}(\alpha)\) is swept from 0 to α.
      • Two-qubit gates: CNOT and CZ are controlled X and Z, replayed as controlled \(X^s\) and \(Z^s\). SWAP is replayed as \(\mathrm{SWAP}^s\), which multiplies the singlet part \((|01\rangle-|10\rangle)/\sqrt2\) of the state by \(e^{i\pi s}\).
      • Bloch vectors: \(\vec r=\langle\psi|\vec\sigma|\psi\rangle\) for one qubit. For two qubits each sphere shows the reduced state \(\rho_A=\mathrm{Tr}_B|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=(I+\vec r_A\cdot\vec\sigma)/2\). The concurrence is \(C=2|c_{00}c_{11}-c_{01}c_{10}|\); for a pure pair \(|\vec r_A|=|\vec r_B|=\sqrt{1-C^2}\), and the entanglement entropy is the binary entropy of \((1\pm|\vec r_A|)/2\).
      • Shots: ideal projective measurements on fresh copies, drawn with the browser's random number generator from the Born-rule probabilities \((1\pm\vec r\cdot\hat n)/2\). Joint outcomes rotate each qubit into the chosen basis first (H for x, then S† and H for y). Error bars are one binomial standard deviation from the measured fraction. With few shots the tomography estimate can land outside the sphere.
      • Checks: the notebook builds the same gates as matrices, takes fractional powers by diagonalization and computes partial traces with NumPy. It agrees with this page to about \(10^{-15}\).
