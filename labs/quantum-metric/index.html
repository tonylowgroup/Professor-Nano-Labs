The Quantum Metric

Geometry starts with measuring distance. Measure it on curved surfaces, where an arrow carried around a loop comes back turned; then between quantum states, where a state on its way round a loop runs up a distance on a quantum odometer; then between the Bloch states of graphene, whose quantum metric piles up at the valleys and blows up as the gap closes.

How far apart are two states?

On a map, a step of one centimetre does not always cover the same distance on the ground: near the poles of a world map, Greenland looks enormous. The metric is the rule that turns steps on the map into true distances, and once you have it, you can work out how curved the surface is without ever leaving it.

Quantum states can be measured the same way. Two states that are nearly the same are close; two that cannot be confused are far apart. Change a crystal's wavevector \(\mathbf k\) a little and its Bloch state moves a little: the quantum metric says how far. Card 1 met its partner, the Berry curvature, which says how much the state twists. Together they are the quantum geometry of the band.

Metric
The rule that turns small steps in your coordinates into real distances. On a flat sheet with a square grid it is trivial; on a curved surface it changes from place to place.
Curvature
How much an arrow, carried round a small loop without ever being turned, has turned when it comes back, per unit area of the loop.
Quantum distance
How different two states are, from their overlap: arccos|⟨ψ|φ⟩| is 0 for the same state and π/2 for states that can never be confused. For nearby states its square is 1 − |⟨ψ|φ⟩|².
Quantum metric
The square of the quantum distance between the states at k and at k + dk, divided by the square of the step: how fast the state changes as k moves.
Berry curvature
The quantum metric's partner from card 1: how much the state's phase twists round a small loop, per unit area.
Quantum geometric tensor
The two together: its real part is the quantum metric, its imaginary part minus half the Berry curvature.
The math

On a surface \(\mathbf r(u^1,u^2)\) the tangent vectors \(\mathbf e_i=\partial\mathbf r/\partial u^i\) define the metric \(g_{ij}=\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j\), and \(ds^2=g_{ij}\,du^i du^j\). For Bloch states \(|u_{\mathbf k}\rangle\), the tangent vectors are \(|D_iu\rangle=(1-P)|\partial_iu\rangle\) with \(P=|u\rangle\langle u|\), and their inner products form the quantum geometric tensor:

$$Q_{ij}=\langle D_iu|D_ju\rangle=\langle\partial_iu|\partial_ju\rangle-A_iA_j=g_{ij}-\tfrac i2\,\Omega_{ij},\qquad 1-\big|\langle u_{\mathbf k}|u_{\mathbf k+d\mathbf k}\rangle\big|^2\approx g_{ij}\,dk_i\,dk_j$$

The quantum metric \(g_{ij}=\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{ij}\) is real and symmetric, like a surface's metric; the Berry curvature \(\Omega_{ij}=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{ij}\) is antisymmetric. Neither depends on the phase chosen for each state.

Show

A map of a curved surface needs a metric to measure it.

Any surface can be drawn, a piece at a time, on a flat chart with two coordinates, like a world map. The metric \(g_{ij}\) turns steps on the chart into distances on the surface: where the chart squeezes the surface, a short step on the chart is a long way on the surface. And the metric alone tells you how curved the surface is: slide an arrow round a small loop without ever turning it, and it comes back turned by the curvature times the area.

What to notice: the small ellipses on the chart are the steps that all cover the same distance on the surface; they are circles only where the chart is true to scale. The arrow comes back turned one way on a sphere, the other way on a saddle, and not at all on a cylinder, which is curved in space but flat to anyone living on it.

The math

With \(\mathbf e_i=\partial\mathbf r/\partial u^i\), \(g_{ij}=\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j\) and \(ds^2=g_{ij}du^idu^j\). An arrow carried along a curve so that it never turns within the surface (\(\nabla\mathbf v=0\), parallel transport) comes back round a loop turned by the total Gaussian curvature inside, \(\delta\alpha=\iint K\,dA\) (Gauss–Bonnet), which is \(K\times\text{area}\) for a small loop. \(K=\det L/\det g\), with \(L_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_j\mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\); Gauss showed it depends on \(g\) alone.

$$ds^2=g_{ij}\,du^i du^j,\qquad \partial_k\mathbf e_i=\Gamma^m_{ki}\,\mathbf e_m+L_{ki}\,\hat{\mathbf n},\qquad \delta\alpha=\oint\!\!\text{turn}=\iint K\,dA\approx K\times\text{area}$$

Quantum states have a distance too.

How different two quantum states are comes down to their overlap \(|\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle|\): 1 for the same state, 0 for states that can never be confused. The quantum distance \(\arccos|\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle|\) runs from 0 to \(\pi/2\); for nearby states its square is \(1-|\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle|^2\), the form in the video. For a spin, the states sit on the Bloch sphere, and this distance turns it into a measured surface: a sphere of radius ½. Send a state round a loop and add up the small steps: that is its quantum odometer.

What to notice: the distance from the start, "as the crow flies", rises and falls; the odometer, "along the road", only grows. Scramble every state's phase: nothing you can measure changes. The loop's length is set by the metric; its Berry phase, from card 1, by the curvature.

The math

For nearby states, \(1-|\langle u|u+du\rangle|^2\approx g_{ij}\,du^idu^j\). For a spin-½ in direction \((\theta,\varphi)\) this gives \(ds^2=\tfrac14\big(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\big)\), a sphere of radius ½: so a circle of latitude has length \(\pi\sin\theta\), and the Fubini–Study distance between any two states is \(\arccos|\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle|\), half the angle between their arrows.

$$d_{\rm FS}(\psi,\varphi)=\arccos\big|\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle\big|,\qquad ds^2=\tfrac14\big(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\big),\qquad Q_{ij}=g_{ij}-\tfrac i2\Omega_{ij}$$

In graphene, the quantum metric piles up at the valleys.

Move along a path in graphene's Brillouin zone and the electron's state, a mix of its two carbon atoms, changes. The quantum metric says how fast, at each \(\mathbf k\). Far from the valleys the state barely changes; at the valleys K and K′, where the gap is smallest, it turns quickly, and the metric is large. Shrink the gap and it becomes larger still.

What to notice: the ellipses on the map show how far the state moves for a small step in each direction, and they swell at K and K′. Halve the gap and the metric at K grows four times; close it, in plain graphene, and the state flips to an orthogonal one as it crosses K: the odometer jumps.

The math

For a two-band model \(H=d_0+\mathbf d\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), with \(\mathbf n=\mathbf d/|\mathbf d|\), the lower band has \(g_{ij}=\tfrac14\,\partial_i\mathbf n\cdot\partial_j\mathbf n\) and \(\Omega_{xy}=\tfrac12\,\mathbf n\cdot(\partial_x\mathbf n\times\partial_y\mathbf n)\). Because \(\partial_x\mathbf n\times\partial_y\mathbf n\) points along \(\mathbf n\), \(\sqrt{\det g}=|\Omega|/2\) exactly, and so \(\mathrm{tr}\,g\ge|\Omega|\). Near a valley, a massive Dirac cone with speed \(v\) and mass \(M\) gives \(\mathrm{tr}\,g=v^2/2M^2\) at K.

$$g_{ij}=\tfrac14\,\partial_i\mathbf n\cdot\partial_j\mathbf n,\qquad \sqrt{\det g}=\tfrac12|\Omega|,\qquad \mathrm{tr}\,g\ \ge\ |\Omega|,\qquad \mathrm{tr}\,g\big|_{K}=\frac{v^2}{2M^2}$$

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · Surfaces

        Carry the arrow round a loop on a cylinder. The cylinder is plainly curved. Does the arrow come back turned?

        Show answer

        No, not at all, wherever the loop is. A cylinder is a rolled-up sheet of paper: you can unroll it flat without stretching or tearing, so distances on it, and its metric, are those of a flat sheet, and its curvature is zero. It is curved in space, but not to anyone living on it, and an arrow slid round a loop cannot tell it from a plane. A sphere cannot be flattened without stretching it, which is why every world map distorts.

        Question 2 · Surfaces

        A loop of size 0.5 turns the arrow by 44° anticlockwise on a sphere of radius 1. Put the same loop at the centre of the saddle, where the curvature is −1. What happens?

        Show answer

        It turns the other way, clockwise, by 38°. On a sphere, the curvature is positive, and the arrow turns the same way the loop goes round; on a saddle it is negative, and it turns against it. A small loop would turn it by curvature × area = 45° either way; these loops are not quite small, and the saddle flattens out away from its centre, so it gives a little less.

        Question 3 · Surfaces

        On the sphere's chart, longitude across and latitude up, like a world map, move the point up to latitude 60°. What shape is the ellipse of equal distances there?

        Show answer

        Twice as wide as it is tall. At latitude 60°, a circle of latitude is only half as long as the equator, \(\cos 60°=\tfrac12\), but the chart draws it just as long; so a degree of longitude covers half the distance of a degree of latitude, \(g_{\varphi\varphi}=\cos^2 60°=\tfrac14\), and the ellipse must stretch across to cover the same distance. That is why Greenland looks so big on a world map.

        Question 4 · Quantum distance

        A state goes round the equator of the Bloch sphere. Half way round, how far is it from where it started? And how far has it travelled?

        Show answer

        As far as two states can be: \(\arccos 0=\pi/2\), with \(1-|\langle\psi_0|\psi\rangle|^2=1\). Half way round the equator the state points the opposite way, and opposite spins can never be confused. On the odometer it has travelled \(\pi/2\) too: the equator is a great circle, so half way round it is also the shortest way. All the way round it is \(\pi\): the equator of a sphere of radius ½. Back at the start its distance from the start is 0 again, but the odometer still reads \(\pi\).

        Question 5 · Quantum distance

        Give every state on the loop a random phase, and press "New random phases" a few times. What changes?

        Show answer

        Only the direction of the arrow in the dial, the phase of the overlap \(\langle\psi_0|\psi\rangle\), which jumps about. Its length does not, so neither does the distance from the start; nor do the odometer and the Berry phase. Distances depend only on \(|\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle|\), so, like the Berry curvature, the quantum metric does not care about the phase you give each state.

        Question 6 · Quantum distance

        Compare loops round a 60° and a 120° cone. Which is longer on the odometer? Which has the larger Berry phase?

        Show answer

        They are exactly as long, \(\pi\sin\theta=2.72\), mirror images on either side of the equator, where the longest of these loops, \(\pi\), runs. But the 120° loop encloses three times the area, so its Berry phase is three times larger, \(-1.5\pi\) against \(-0.5\pi\). (A phase only counts up to whole turns of \(2\pi\), so \(-1.5\pi\) is the same as \(+0.5\pi\): the two twists are equal and opposite.) The metric measures the loop's length, the curvature the area inside it: the real and imaginary parts of one tensor.

        Question 7 · Graphene

        Graphene on a substrate with \(M=0.2\,t\): the quantum metric at the valley K is \(\mathrm{tr}\,g=28\). Halve the mass to \(0.1\,t\). What does it become?

        Show answer

        Four times larger, 112.5. Near K the electron is a massive Dirac particle. At K its state sits wholly on one carbon atom, and it tilts toward an equal share of both over a distance of about \(M/v\) in \(\mathbf k\), with \(v=\tfrac32 t\): halve the mass and it turns twice as fast, and the metric, a speed squared, grows four times: \(\mathrm{tr}\,g=v^2/2M^2\). As the gap closes the metric blows up, one of the signatures of a quantum phase transition.

        Question 8 · GrapheneChallenge

        Plain graphene, with no gap, and the path straight across valley K. What does the odometer do as the path crosses K?

        Show answer

        It jumps by \(\pi/2\), the largest step there is. Without a gap the electron's state hardly changes near K along this line, but on the far side of K it is a different, orthogonal state: crossing K it flips across the Bloch sphere at once, and \(\arccos 0=\pi/2\). The metric is infinite at K: along this path, that whole \(\pi/2\) is concentrated at the single point K. Give graphene a small gap and the jump is smoothed out over a distance of about \(M/v\).

        What is being computed?
        • The surfaces: a plane, \(\mathbf r=(x,y,0)\); a cylinder of radius 1, \((\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,h)\); a sphere of radius 1 in longitude \(\varphi\) and latitude \(\lambda\), \((\cos\lambda\cos\varphi,\cos\lambda\sin\varphi,\sin\lambda)\); and a saddle, \(z=(x^2-y^2)/2\). The tangent vectors and the metric come from central differences of \(\mathbf r\); the Gaussian curvature from \(K=\det L/\det g\), with the second fundamental form \(L_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_j\mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\). On the saddle \(K=-1/(1+x^2+y^2)^2\).
        • The loop: the metric ellipse \(g_{ij}\,du^idu^j=\varepsilon^2\) around the point, which to first order is a circle of radius \(\varepsilon\) on the surface; it is shrunk if needed to stay on the chart. The arrow starts along \(\mathbf e_1\) and is carried round 400 points: at each step it is rotated with the surface's normal, by the smallest rotation taking one normal to the next, so it never turns within the surface (the Levi-Civita parallel transport of the surface). Its turn is compared with \(\iint K\,dA\) over the inside of the loop (Gauss–Bonnet), by Gauss–Legendre quadrature, and with \(K\times\text{area}\), the small-loop value.
        • Quantum distances: for spin-½ states \(|{+}\mathbf n\rangle\) on a circle of latitude, the distance from the start is the Fubini–Study distance \(\arccos|\langle\psi_0|\psi\rangle|\), and the odometer adds up \(\arccos|\langle\psi_j|\psi_{j+1}\rangle|\) over 400 steps: the shortest distance for each step, so it cuts the loop's curve very slightly short of its length, \(\pi\sin\theta\). "Scrambled" multiplies each state by a random phase. The Berry phase is \(-\tfrac12\) the solid angle the loop encloses on the unit Bloch sphere, \(-\pi(1-\cos\theta)\), as in card 1.
        • Graphene: the same model as card 1, with carbon–carbon distance 1 and hopping \(t=1\): \(d_x+id_y=\sum_je^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j}\), a sublattice mass \(M\) and Haldane's \(t_2e^{\pm i\varphi}\). The quantum metric of the lower band is \(g_{ij}=\tfrac14\partial_i\mathbf n\cdot\partial_j\mathbf n\), from analytic derivatives of \(\mathbf d\); it agrees with \(1-|\langle u_{\mathbf k}|u_{\mathbf k+\delta\mathbf k}\rangle|^2\) for small steps. Each ellipse on the map has axes along the eigenvectors of \(g\), proportional to the square roots of its eigenvalues: the distance the state moves for a step of fixed size in each direction. The odometer adds up \(\arccos|\langle u_j|u_{j+1}\rangle|\) along the path, 1200 steps.
        • Checks: with 400 steps, the arrow's turn agrees with \(\iint K\,dA\) to about \(10^{-4}\) rad, and loop lengths on the Bloch sphere with \(\pi\sin\theta\) to about \(10^{-5}\); both errors shrink as the steps get smaller. \(\sqrt{\det g}\) agrees with \(|\Omega|/2\) to \(10^{-12}\), and \(\mathrm{tr}\,g\) at K with \(v^2/2M^2\) to rounding error. The notebook repeats the calculation independently and asserts every number quoted in the answers.
