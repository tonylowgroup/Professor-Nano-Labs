Trapping an Ion: The Paul Trap

No arrangement of fixed charges can hold an ion still: Earnshaw's theorem says it will always roll off a saddle. Flip the saddle fast enough and the ion stays. Watch it happen, map every trap you could build, then line ions up into a crystal whose vibrations become the phonons of a quantum computer.

One ion, four rods and two end caps

The ion is \(^{40}\mathrm{Ca}^+\), the same ion as in the trapped-ion lab. In a linear Paul trap, four rods run parallel to the trap axis \(z\), each a distance \(r_0=0.5\) mm from it. A voltage \(U_{\rm DC}-V\cos\Omega_{\rm RF}t\) is applied between the two pairs of opposite rods: a radio-frequency part \(V\), and perhaps a steady part \(U_{\rm DC}\). Near the axis, the ion's potential energy in the \(x\)–\(y\) plane is a saddle whose sign follows the voltage:

$$U(x,y,t)=\frac{e}{2r_0^2}\big(U_{\rm DC}-V\cos\Omega_{\rm RF}t\big)\,(x^2-y^2)$$

Two electrodes at the ends of the rods, the end caps, hold the ion along \(z\). The four tabs follow the video: why a fixed field cannot trap, how the oscillating field does, which voltages work, and what happens when many ions share the trap.

A saddle, never a bowl.

In empty space the electric potential obeys Laplace's equation. Near the trap centre that leaves the ion's potential energy with three curvatures that must add up to zero. Choose two of them; Laplace's equation fixes the third. At least one direction always slopes downhill, and the ion rolls out along it. This is Earnshaw's theorem.

$$\nabla^2\Phi=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad U=\tfrac12 m\omega_0^2\left(k_x x^2+k_y y^2+k_z z^2\right),\qquad k_x+k_y+k_z=0$$

Flip the saddle before the ion can roll off.

With the radio-frequency voltage on, the saddle turns inside out twice every RF period: the direction that was downhill becomes uphill. If it flips fast enough, the ion is pushed back each time before it gets away. It moves in a slow swing, the secular motion, with a small fast jiggle on top, the micromotion. On average it behaves as if it sat in a bowl.

$$\begin{gathered}\ddot x-\frac{eV}{m r_0^2}\cos(\Omega_{\rm RF}t)\,x=0,\qquad q=\frac{2eV}{m r_0^2\Omega_{\rm RF}^2}\\ x(t)\approx x_1\cos(\omega t)\Big[1-\frac q2\cos\Omega_{\rm RF}t\Big],\qquad \omega\approx\frac{q\,\Omega_{\rm RF}}{2\sqrt2}\end{gathered}$$

Every trap you could build, on one map.

Rescale time as \(\xi=\Omega_{\rm RF}t/2\), so that one RF period is \(\xi=\pi\), and the motion along each direction becomes the Mathieu equation, with two numbers: \(q\) from the RF voltage and \(a\) from the steady voltage. Along \(x\) the ion feels \((a,q)\), along \(y\) it feels \((-a,-q)\). The ion is trapped only where both are stable. Click anywhere on the chart to build that trap.

$$\frac{d^2u}{d\xi^2}+\big(a-2q\cos2\xi\big)u=0,\qquad \xi=\frac{\Omega_{\rm RF}t}2,\qquad a=\frac{4eU_{\rm DC}}{m r_0^2\Omega_{\rm RF}^2},\qquad \omega=\beta\,\frac{\Omega_{\rm RF}}2,\quad \beta\approx\sqrt{a+q^2/2}$$

Many ions: a string held apart by its own repulsion.

The end caps make a bowl along the axis at frequency \(\omega_z\); the RF makes a stiffer one across it at \(\omega_r\). Load several ions and they push each other apart until the bowl and the repulsion balance. Cooled, they freeze into a string, or a zigzag if the radial grip is too weak. The string vibrates in normal modes, and those are the phonons that the trapped-ion lab uses as its bus.

$$U=\sum_i\tfrac12 m\left(\omega_z^2z_i^2+\omega_r^2x_i^2\right)+\sum_{i\lt j}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{ij}},\qquad \ell=\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 m\omega_z^2}\right)^{1/3},\qquad \omega_{\rm COM}=\omega_z,\quad \omega_{\rm breathing}=\sqrt3\,\omega_z$$

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · No static trap

        Make the potential a bowl in the x–y plane, \(k_x=k_y=\tfrac12\). Is the ion trapped now?

        Show answer

        No. Laplace's equation forces \(k_z=-1\), a hill along z. Switch to the x–z plane: the ion swings back and forth along x but rolls out along z, reaching the electrodes at \(t\approx2.8/\omega_0\). Any bowl in two directions makes a hill in the third. Reverse the signs and you have the end-cap field of a linear trap, which holds along z and pushes outward across it.

        Question 2 · Flip the saddle

        Flip the saddle slowly: 300 V at 5 MHz. Does the ion stay?

        Show answer

        No. Then \(q\approx5.9\), far beyond the stable limit of 0.908. The saddle holds still long enough for the ion to roll away, and it reaches the electrodes within a fifth of an RF period. At 300 V the drive must be faster than about 12.7 MHz; at 20 MHz, \(q=0.37\) and the ion is held.

        Question 3 · Flip the saddle

        The ion swings slowly, with a fast jiggle on top. How many RF periods make one slow swing, and how big is the jiggle?

        Show answer

        About 7.5. The secular frequency is \(\beta\,\Omega_{\rm RF}/2\) with \(\beta=0.267\), so a swing lasts \(2/\beta\approx7.5\) RF periods: 2.67 MHz, against 2.59 MHz from the simple bowl formula. The jiggle, the micromotion, is about \(q/2\approx18\%\) of the swing, and it shrinks to nothing as the ion approaches the centre.

        Question 4 · Flip the saddle

        Double the RF frequency at the same voltage. What happens to \(q\), to the secular frequency and to the trap depth?

        Show answer

        \(q\) falls to a quarter, 0.092, because it goes as \(1/\Omega_{\rm RF}^2\). The secular frequency, about \(q\Omega_{\rm RF}/2\sqrt2\propto V/\Omega_{\rm RF}\), halves to 1.30 MHz. The depth \(qV/8\) drops from 13.8 eV to 3.4 eV. Flipping faster is safer but makes the bowl shallower, so real traps raise the voltage along with the frequency.

        Question 5 · Stability chart

        Raise \(q\) along \(a=0\). Where does trapping fail, and what happens to the secular frequency just before?

        Show answer

        At \(q=0.908\). On the way, \(\beta\) climbs toward 1: the slow swing speeds up until it reaches half the drive frequency, \(\Omega_{\rm RF}/2\), and locks to it. At \(q=0.9\), \(\beta=0.916\), while the bowl formula \(q/\sqrt2\) would give only 0.64: the simple picture fails long before the edge.

        Question 6 · Stability chart

        Add a steady voltage, \(a=0.1\), at \(q=0.4\). Is the ion still trapped?

        Show answer

        No. The steady voltage adds a static saddle. Along x it stiffens the trap, \(\beta_x=0.44\). Along y it adds a hill, \(-a\), that beats the RF bowl, \(q^2/2=0.08\), so y is lost. At \(q=0.4\) the steady part must stay within \(|a|<0.079\). A quadrupole mass filter works near the tip of this region: only a narrow band of masses has both directions stable.

        Question 7 · Ion crystals

        Two ions with \(\omega_z=2\pi\times1\) MHz. How far apart do they sit, and at what frequencies do they vibrate along the axis? Then add more ions: what happens to the two lowest modes?

        Show answer

        They sit \(2^{1/3}\ell\approx5.6\ \mu\)m apart, with \(\ell=4.45\ \mu\)m. Together they swing at \(\omega_z\) (the centre-of-mass mode, 1 MHz) and against each other at \(\sqrt3\,\omega_z\) (the breathing mode, 1.73 MHz). As long as the ions stay in a straight string, these two modes along the axis stay at exactly 1 and \(\sqrt3\) times \(\omega_z\) for any number of ions, and every new axial mode lies above them. (At this radial grip, 9 or more ions buckle into a zigzag, and then the breathing mode is gone.)

        Question 8 · Ion crystals

        Ten ions, with \(\omega_z=2\pi\times1\) MHz and \(\omega_r=2\pi\times4\) MHz. A straight string, or not? How strong must the radial grip be to keep them in a line?

        Show answer

        Not straight: a zigzag. The middle of the string is crowded, and the ions there escape sideways. They stay in a line only while \(\omega_r>4.60\,\omega_z\). As \(\omega_r\) falls toward that value, the lowest radial mode (the zigzag mode) slows to zero frequency: the string has lost its stiffness sideways.

        What is being computed?
        • No static trap: the ion starts at rest, 0.12 of the way to the electrodes along each axis. Along each axis it moves as \(\cos(\sqrt k\,\omega_0t)\) when \(k>0\) and as \(\cosh(\sqrt{-k}\,\omega_0t)\) when \(k<0\). It is lost when it reaches the electrodes.
        • Flip the saddle: the exact equation of motion, the Mathieu equation, is integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta, 120 steps per RF period (240 when \(q>2\)). The ion starts at rest at \(x=0.3\,r_0,\ y=0.2\,r_0\) at the peak of the RF and is lost if it reaches \(r=r_0\). Near the edge of stability the swing and its jiggle would reach the electrodes from there, so the start is scaled down until the largest excursion is \(0.8\,r_0\). Only the first stability region, \(q<0.908\), counts as a trap. The dashed curve is the slow swing alone, \(x_1\cos\omega t\), with the bowl-picture frequency \(\omega=\sqrt{a+q^2/2}\,\Omega_{\rm RF}/2\).
        • Ion and trap: \(^{40}\)Ca\(^+\), mass 39.96 u, \(r_0=0.5\) mm. The trap depth is the bowl picture's \(qV/8\) (in eV, for a singly charged ion, at \(a=0\)).
        • Stability chart: for each \((a,q)\) the one-period transfer matrix of the Mathieu equation is integrated; the motion is bounded when its trace lies between −2 and 2, and then \(\cos\pi\beta\) is half the trace. The edges of the region are found by bisection and agree with SciPy's Mathieu characteristic values \(a_0(q)\) and \(b_1(q)\) to about \(10^{-7}\).
        • Ion crystals: the ions move in the x–z plane, with the RF averaged into a radial bowl at \(\omega_r\). The equilibrium is found by minimising the energy (Newton's method, which steps away from a straight string when it is unstable), and the normal modes are the eigenvectors of the energy's second derivatives. "Load hot ions" integrates the motion with a friction term standing in for laser cooling.
        • Checks: the stability edges, \(\beta\), trajectories, ion positions and mode frequencies agree with independent SciPy calculations to better than \(10^{-6}\).
