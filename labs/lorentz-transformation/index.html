The Lorentz Transformation: Clocks, Trains and Light

Maxwell's equations give light one speed, and Einstein took that literally: the speed of light is the same for everyone, however they move. Watch what has to give. A moving clock ticks slowly, a moving train is short, two flashes at the same time on the platform are not at the same time on the train, and no two speeds add up to more than light.

One speed for everyone

Maxwell's equations predict waves of electric and magnetic field travelling at \(c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\approx3\times10^8\) m/s: light. They do not say relative to what. Einstein's answer, in 1905, was: relative to anyone. Every observer moving steadily measures the same \(c\), and the laws of physics look the same to all of them.

That one rule forces space and time to mix. If you and I move relative to each other, my clocks run slow by your measure, my metre sticks are short, and what is simultaneous for me is not for you; and the other way round. The rule that converts my coordinates of an event into yours is the Lorentz transformation. In this lab, \(c=1\): times in seconds go with distances in light-seconds.

Frame
An observer's set of rulers and synchronised clocks, all moving together at a steady speed: the platform's, or the train's.
Event
Something that happens at one place at one time: a lightning strike, a tick of a clock. Each frame gives it a place x and a time t.
Lorentz factor
γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²): 1 at rest, 1.25 at 0.6c, 10 at 0.995c, without limit as v approaches c.
Time dilation
A moving clock runs slow by a factor γ, measured by clocks it moves past.
Length contraction
A moving object is shorter along its motion by a factor γ, measured in the frame it moves through.
Relativity of simultaneity
Two events at the same time in one frame happen at different times in a frame moving along the line between them.
The math

For a frame moving at speed \(v\) along \(x\), an event at \((x,t)\) has coordinates

$$x'=\gamma\,(x-vt),\qquad t'=\gamma\Big(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\Big),\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}},\qquad c^2t'^2-x'^2=c^2t^2-x^2$$

The last equation is what stays the same: the spacetime interval between the event and the origin; for any two events, use the differences \(\Delta t\) and \(\Delta x\). (The lab's train tab writes it the other way round, \((\Delta x)^2-c^2(\Delta t)^2\), which is just as invariant.) A light ray, \(x=ct\), has interval zero in every frame, so it moves at \(c\) in every frame. At low speeds \(\gamma\approx1\) and \(vx/c^2\approx0\), and the transformation becomes Galileo's, \(x'=x-vt\), \(t'=t\).

Show

A moving clock ticks slowly.

The simplest clock: a flash of light bouncing between two mirrors, one tick for each trip up and back. Put one clock on the platform and send an identical one past at speed \(v\). Seen from the platform, the moving clock's light has to travel along a diagonal to keep up with its mirrors. The diagonal is longer, light is no faster, so each tick takes longer.

What to notice: at everyday speeds the two clocks keep step. At \(0.6c\) the moving clock makes 8 ticks for every 10 of the platform clock; near light it almost stops. Muons made high in the atmosphere live only 2.2 μs, too short to reach the ground at any speed, unless their clocks run slow.

The math

On the platform, half a tick of the moving clock takes \(\Delta t/2\): the light goes \(c\Delta t/2\) along the diagonal while the mirrors move \(v\Delta t/2\) sideways, and the mirrors are \(L\) apart. Pythagoras:

$$\Big(\frac{c\Delta t}{2}\Big)^2=L^2+\Big(\frac{v\Delta t}{2}\Big)^2\;\Rightarrow\;\Delta t=\frac{2L/c}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}=\gamma\,\Delta\tau$$

where \(\Delta\tau=2L/c\) is the tick of the clock at rest. A muon with lifetime \(\tau\) travels \(\gamma v\tau\) on average before it decays, and the fraction that survive a distance \(d\) is \(e^{-d/\gamma v\tau}\).

Same time on the platform, not on the train.

Lightning strikes both ends of a moving train, at the same moment as judged on the platform. Light from the two strikes reaches an observer standing on the platform midway between them at the same time, so the platform says: simultaneous. But a passenger in the middle of the train is rushing towards the front flash and away from the back one, and sees the front flash first. Light moves at \(c\) for her too, and she sits in the middle of the train, so she concludes: the front was struck first.

What to notice: in the spacetime diagram, each frame's "now" is a line. The platform's lines are level; the train's are tilted, and the faster the train, the more they tilt. Switch to the train's view: now the train is at rest, its strikes are at different times, and the platform's clocks are the ones out of step.

The math

On the platform the train, of rest length \(L_0\), is \(L=L_0/\gamma\) long, and the strikes are at \(x=\mp L/2\), \(t=0\). In the train's frame, with \(t'=\gamma(t-vx/c^2)\),

$$t'_{\rm back}-t'_{\rm front}=\gamma\,\frac{vL}{c^2}=\frac{vL_0}{c^2},\qquad x'_{\rm front}-x'_{\rm back}=\gamma L=L_0,\qquad (\Delta x')^2-c^2(\Delta t')^2=L^2$$

The front strike comes first by \(vL_0/c^2\), and the strikes are a full train length apart, as they must be for someone on board. The interval is the same in both frames.

Speeds don't simply add.

Throw a ball forwards at \(u'\) inside a train that moves at \(v\). Common sense says the platform sees the ball at \(u'+v\). That is very nearly right for balls and trains, and badly wrong near the speed of light: the platform sees \((u'+v)/(1+u'v/c^2)\), which never exceeds \(c\). Shine a torch forwards instead of throwing a ball, and the platform sees its light move at exactly \(c\).

What to notice: in the spacetime diagram, common sense would send the ball onto or beyond the light lines; relativity bends it back inside. What does add simply is the rapidity, \(\phi\) with \(v=c\tanh\phi\): equal pushes add equal rapidity, but each brings a smaller gain in speed.

The math

The ball's path in the train is \(x'=u't'\). Transform back to the platform with \(x=\gamma(x'+vt')\), \(t=\gamma(t'+vx'/c^2)\), and divide:

$$u=\frac{x}{t}=\frac{u'+v}{1+u'v/c^2},\qquad \tanh^{-1}\frac{u}{c}=\tanh^{-1}\frac{u'}{c}+\tanh^{-1}\frac{v}{c}$$

With \(u'=c\), \(u=(c+v)/(1+v/c)=c\), for any \(v\).

        Step 2 · Predict, then test

        Predict first

        Load each example, make a prediction, then check it in the lab.

        Question 1 · Light clock

        The moving clock passes at \(0.6c\). While the platform clock ticks 10 times, how many times does the moving clock tick?

        Show answer

        8. At \(0.6c\), \(\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-0.36}=1/0.8=1.25\), so each tick of the moving clock takes 1.25 platform ticks, and 10 platform ticks hold \(10/1.25=8\) of them. In the light triangle, for each half tick the light goes 1.25 mirror gaps along the diagonal while the clock moves 0.75 gaps sideways: \(1.25^2=1^2+0.75^2\).

        Question 2 · Light clock

        At \(0.8c\), someone riding with the moving clock compares it with the platform clock. Whose clock does she measure running slow?

        Show answer

        The platform's, by the same factor, \(\gamma=5/3\): by her measurement, the platform clock makes 6 ticks while hers makes 10. (What she literally sees is different again: light from a clock she is moving away from arrives ever later, so it seems to tick at only a third of her rate; 6 is what she finds once she allows for the light's travel time.) Both are right. Each compares one passing clock with two of their own, at two places, and their own two clocks are synchronised only by their own standard of "at the same time". Tab 2 shows how the two standards differ.

        Question 3 · Light clock

        Muons are made 15 km up, by cosmic rays, and come down at \(0.995c\). They live 2.2 μs on average. How far does a typical muon get, and what fraction reach the ground?

        Show answer

        Without time dilation, \(0.995\,c\times2.197\) μs is only 655 m, and the fraction reaching the ground would be \(e^{-15000/655}\approx10^{-10}\): none. With \(\gamma=10.0\), their clocks run ten times slow; a typical muon travels 6.56 km, and \(e^{-15/6.56}\approx10\%\) would reach the ground. That is an idealised figure: real muons are made at many heights and energies, and they slow down as they lose energy in the air. But plenty of them do reach sea level, as Rossi and Hall (1941) and Frisch and Smith (1963) measured, which is impossible without time dilation. Seen by the muon, its clock is fine: the atmosphere is contracted to 1.5 km.

        Question 4 · Simultaneity

        The strikes are simultaneous on the platform. For the passenger in the middle of the train, which end was struck first, and by how much?

        Show answer

        The front, by \(vL_0/c^2=0.6\) s for a train one light-second long. She sees the front flash at 0.25 s and the back flash at 1 s (platform time); she knows both flashes travelled half a train length at \(c\) to reach her, so she concludes the front strike really did happen first. Switch to the train's view: there the train's "now" lines through the two strikes are level, and 0.6 s apart.

        Question 5 · Length

        At \(0.8c\), how long is the train, one light-second long at rest, as measured on the platform? Does the passenger agree?

        Show answer

        On the platform it is \(L_0/\gamma=1/(5/3)=0.6\) light-seconds long: the strikes that hit its two ends at the same platform time are 0.6 apart. The passenger disagrees: for her the train is 1 long, and the strikes were not simultaneous. The front was struck 0.8 s before the back, while the platform was sliding past, so the platform marks are what she finds contracted.

        Question 6 · The interval

        Platform and passenger disagree about the time and the distance between the two strikes. Is there anything they agree on? Could anyone see both strikes at the same place?

        Show answer

        They agree on the interval \((\Delta x)^2-c^2(\Delta t)^2\): at \(0.6c\), the platform says \(0.8^2-0=0.64\), the passenger \(1^2-0.6^2=0.64\). Every observer's pair \((\Delta x,c\Delta t)\) lies on the same hyperbola, so no one sees the strikes less than 0.8 apart, and no one sees them at the same place. Observers moving the other way along the platform see the back strike first: the order of these events depends on the frame, which is safe, because neither can cause the other.

        Question 7 · Adding

        The train moves at \(0.5c\), and the ball is thrown forwards at \(0.5c\) inside it. How fast does the ball move, seen from the platform?

        Show answer

        \(0.8c\), not \(c\): \((0.5+0.5)/(1+0.25)=0.8\). In rapidities it does add: \(\tanh^{-1}0.5=0.549\), twice that is 1.099, and \(\tanh1.099=0.8\). Two pushes of \(0.9c\) give \(0.9945c\): the closer you get to \(c\), the less each push gains.

        Question 8 · AddingChallenge

        The train moves at \(0.9c\) and a passenger shines a torch forwards. How fast does the platform see the light go?

        Show answer

        Exactly \(c\): \((1+0.9)/(1+0.9)=1\). Common sense says \(1.9c\). This is the postulate Einstein started from in 1905, alongside the principle that the laws of physics are the same for every steadily moving observer: light from a moving source still moves at \(c\), measured from anywhere. It was later tested directly, for example in 1964 with gamma rays from particles moving at \(0.99975c\). Everything else in this lab, the slow clocks, the short trains, the tilted "now", follows from those two principles.
